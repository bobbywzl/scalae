## Concepts
- **title**: Cheap and Good (combined value + quality ranking) — question: Does the stock rank well on both an absolute cheapness measure and a return-on-capital quality measure, rather than on just one?, test: Sort candidates by a combined decile of earnings yield and return on capital; prefer top-decile combined names over names that are merely cheap or merely high-quality in isolation, evidence: Greenblatt's own backtest showed decile-one (cheap+good) beating decile-two, decile-two beating decile-three, "and so on in order" down through decile ten
  > "those companies that were in the top decile, based on quantitative measures indicating that they were both cheap and good, performed better than those in the second decile, which performed better than those in the third, and so on in order." @line 827-836

- **title**: Ownership-Stake Framing for Concentration — question: Would I be comfortable holding this position through an unrelated macro scare because I trust the long-run earning power of this specific business?, test: Size positions as if buying a real, private local business rather than a liquid quote that bounces daily; concentration is acceptable only when each name has been researched carefully, evidence: Greenblatt held six to eight positions as 80%+ of his portfolio precisely because he treated them as ownership stakes, not paper
  > "people would say 'how can you own only six or eight companies,' because during a lot of my career, six or eight positions represented 80+% of my portfolio." @line 1256-1261

- **title**: Time Arbitrage via Institutional Agency Problem — question: Is this mispricing durable because the capital that would correct it is structurally judged on a 1-3 year lookback rather than free to wait for 2-3 year intrinsic-value convergence?, test: Identify situations where asset-allocation decision-makers (who are not the ones investing) cannot observe manager skill except through short-term performance, keeping patient capital structurally scarce, evidence: Greenblatt ties the durability of the value edge directly to this agency problem and calls time arbitrage the "last man standing" inefficiency
  > "I think time arbitrage will be the "last man standing," pretty clearly." @line 764-767

- **title**: Asymmetric, Downside-Protected Sizing — question: Is the downside on this position genuinely capped even where the upside is hard to quantify, such that being wrong is cheap?, test: Load capital into positions with well-protected downside rather than into the single highest theoretical-payoff position; not-losing-money is treated as the primary driver of good risk/reward, evidence: Greenblatt states plainly that limited-downside positions, not biggest-payoff positions, are what he loaded up on
  > "Not losing money is a good way to ensure that your portfolio has a good risk/reward profile." @line 1118-1121

- **title**: Time Arbitrage vs. Traditional Arbitrage — question: Am I mispricing the risk of a "sure thing" by expecting classic riskless-arbitrage speed and certainty from what is actually a slower, noisier convergence trade?, test: Frame the trade honestly as time arbitrage — a bet that could take up to three years, bounce randomly in the interim, and still be right — rather than as the instant, riskless gold-arbitrage textbook case, evidence: Greenblatt explicitly contrasts classic buy-gold-in-New-York/sell-in-London arbitrage against his own value bets that can take years and go against you first
  > "The traditional definition of arbitrage always went something like this: buy gold in New York and sell it simultaneously in London, and you'll make a dollar." @line 1233-1236

- **title**: Selective Pass-Rate Investing (only a few of many ideas need to work) — question: Given how many ideas I looked at and passed on, does the portfolio I actually built only need a handful of them to work out for the whole approach to succeed?, test: Look at many candidates (40-50), analyze a manageable subset (six to eight) deeply enough to have real conviction, and accept that only four or five of them need to play out correctly if margin of safety is present on each, evidence: Greenblatt describes passing on the majority of the 40-50 ideas he examines and being fine ending up buying just one, as long as the ones chosen work
  > "If I looked at 40 or 50 ideas, and, while perhaps 12 or 13 of them would have worked out, if I end up only buying one, that's okay." @line 1176-1183

- **title**: Learning From Mistakes, Not Successes — question: Am I extracting the lesson from what went wrong, since that — not what went right — is where the durable improvement in judgment actually comes from?, test: Treat errors as the primary teaching material; deliberately risk being wrong early (when mistakes are cheap) rather than avoiding all risk of error, evidence: Greenblatt says plainly that you don't learn from your successes nearly as much as from what you screwed up, and that you can't be a good investor without investing
  > "unfortunately you don't learn from your successes all that much; you learn from the things you screwed up." @line 1382-1386

- **title**: Thought-Process Screening for People, Not Just Stocks — question: When evaluating a person (co-investor, seeded manager, student) rather than a security, am I judging the clarity and rigor of their thought process rather than their as-yet-unproven track record?, test: Listen to how an investment pitch is built — were the right questions asked, is the reasoning clear — and use that as the forward-looking signal, since a track record doesn't exist yet for someone just starting out, evidence: Greenblatt seeded Robert Goldstein, Brian Gaines and others based on thought process observed before any track record existed
  > "I really just look at thought process. I found them before they had a track record, right?" @line 1433-1436

## Metrics
- **name**: Free Cash Flow Yield Percentile (Russell 1000, bottoms-up) — formula: Market-cap-weighted free cash flow yield of the Russell 1000, built stock-by-stock (bottoms-up) each day, ranked against its own trailing ~23-year distribution to produce a cheap/expensive percentile, reading: 87th percentile toward cheap as of the interview — cheaper only 13% of the time in 23 years, historically associated with about 17% forward one-year and roughly mid-30s% cumulative two-year returns, benchNotes: Bottoms-up construction is the point (avoids mega-cap/index-composition distortion); used to set overall long tilt, not for single-stock selection
  > "we're in about the 87th percentile towards cheap, meaning that the market as measured by the Russell 1000 on a free cash flow basis has only been cheaper 13% of the time over the last 23 years." @line 1083-1091

- **name**: Magic Formula Decile Rank (earnings yield x return on capital) — formula: Rank the universe on a combined score of cheapness (earnings yield) and quality (return on capital) and sort into deciles, decile one being the best combined cheap-and-good score and decile ten the worst, reading: Decile one beat decile two, decile two beat decile three, "and so on in order" through decile ten in Greenblatt's original test, benchNotes: The tested, working use is going long decile one; a pure decile-one-long/decile-ten-short pairing, held a full year without rebalancing, failed badly around Q1 2000 when the book's own afterword test lost all of its money
  > "the results showed that you couldn't figure out a compounded rate of return because you lost all of your money." @line 1040-1044

- **name**: Position Concentration (six-to-eight names ≈ 80%+ of portfolio) — formula: Concentrate capital in the six-to-eight highest-conviction, most-thoroughly-researched ideas, sized so this core represents the large majority of invested capital, reading: "six or eight positions represented 80+% of my portfolio" through a large part of Greenblatt's career, benchNotes: Justified only when each name has been researched carefully, with strong management and good franchises — framed explicitly as conservative rather than as concentration for its own sake
  > "people would say 'how can you own only six or eight companies,' because during a lot of my career, six or eight positions represented 80+% of my portfolio." @line 1256-1261

- **name**: Forward Index Return Conditional on FCF-Yield Percentile — formula: Historical average forward Russell 1000 total return, one year and two years out, conditioned on the index's current bottoms-up FCF-yield percentile bucket, reading: When as cheap as the ~87th percentile, forward one-year return has averaged about 17% and roughly the mid-30s% cumulative over two years; even at the 50th percentile the historical expectation is 8-9%, benchNotes: A market-timing overlay on top of (not a substitute for) bottom-up stock selection — used to set net long/short tilt, and Greenblatt is explicit the forward period could undershoot its historical average
  > "When it has been this cheap, the forward return for the Russell has been about 17% and then about the mid-30's two years out." @line 1101-1105

## Other
- **origin story — the Forbes article on Ben Graham**: Greenblatt traces his entire investing path to a single Forbes article he read as a Wharton junior describing Graham's market-beating formula, Graham's thought process, and "Mr. Market" — a "light bulb" moment that redirected him away from the efficient-market theory Wharton was teaching.
  > "I went to Wharton, and, as they still do today, they taught the efficient market theory. This didn't resonate with me all that well." @line 345-349
- **teaching anecdote — the "I make a guarantee" opening-day line**: Every semester Greenblatt opens class with a deliberately provocative promise that the market will eventually agree with good valuation work — paired immediately with the honest caveat that "eventually" is unpredictable, sometimes years away. It is his signature framing device for teaching patience alongside conviction.
  > "I make a guarantee the first day of class every year that if you're good at valuing companies, the market will agree with you." @line 611-618
- **track-record color — the magic formula decile backtest and its long/short "afterword" failure**: Greenblatt's own systematic test of combined cheapness-and-quality deciles became the empirical basis for The Little Book That Beats the Market, but when he (and countless readers who emailed him) tried the "obvious" refinement — buy decile one, short decile ten — the unrebalanced experiment blew up in the first quarter of 2000, illustrating in his own numbers why a formula that "worked every day" would stop working the moment everyone used it.
  > "the results showed that you couldn't figure out a compounded rate of return because you lost all of your money." @line 1049-1053
- **personal history — giving back through Success Academy Charter Schools**: Greenblatt frames his charter-school philanthropy explicitly as "teaching a man to fish" applied to education, treating the schools like a replicable business prototype (same kids, same or less money as the state, refined and repeated across dozens of campuses) rather than a one-off act of charity.
  > "It really goes back to teaching a man to fish. You want to give back in a way that's leveraged" @line 1613-1616
- **memorable framing — stocks as ownership stakes, not paper**: Greenblatt's core answer to the "isn't six-to-eight stocks too concentrated" objection is a reframing device he returns to repeatedly: stocks are not pieces of paper that bounce around but literal ownership stakes in real businesses, the same mental model as buying into six or eight shops in a town.
  > "I look at them as ownership stakes in businesses." @line 1268-1270

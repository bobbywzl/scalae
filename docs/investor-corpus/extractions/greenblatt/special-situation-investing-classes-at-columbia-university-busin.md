## Question patterns
- **pattern**: Is this a good business - does it earn a high pre-tax return on tangible invested capital, EBIT/(NWC+NFA)? — askWhen: initial screen of any candidate before valuing it, anchor: business-model
  > "The first thing I look at ROIC = EBIT/ (NWC + Net Equipment). How good a business is this?" @line 324
- **pattern**: Is the current weakness/cheapness a temporary problem or a permanent one? — askWhen: a stock looks statistically cheap because of depressed near-term earnings, anchor: business-model
  > "The way you can add value is to distinguish between temporary and permanent problems." @line 9156
- **pattern**: What are normalized earnings likely to be two to three years out, once current abnormal conditions clear, rather than last year's or next quarter's reported number? — askWhen: any valuation exercise, anchor: business-model
  > "I am really looking at normalized EBIT three or four years out vs. last year's EBIT." @line 714
- **pattern**: Does the company have an identifiable barrier to entry (patent, brand, scale, switching cost, location/regulatory edge) that would let it sustain returns above its cost of capital? — askWhen: assessing whether a moat is durable, anchor: business-model
  > "I would define a good business where you can identify specifically a reason why it should be able to earn an excess return on its cost of capital." @line 1152
- **pattern**: If returns have been unusually high or low for several years, is the business reverting to the mean, and is management taking the obvious corrective action? — askWhen: evaluating ROE/ROIC trend sustainability, anchor: business-model
  > "The companies with the highest profitability decline while the lowest rise-convergence to a mean." @line 986
- **pattern**: Would the pieces of this business be worth more sold off or broken up than the whole trades for today? — askWhen: multi-division or conglomerate companies, anchor: business-model
  > "A company has two divisions one is making $3 and the other is losing $1 (EPS = $3 - $1 = $2)." @line 223
- **pattern**: How is management's "bread buttered" - do they hold meaningful equity relative to salary, or mostly cash compensation with little skin in the game? — askWhen: evaluating any management team, anchor: corporate-culture
  > "You want to see how the management's bread buttered. How much of their salary vs. share ownership?" @line 752
- **pattern**: For a spin-off, do insiders/management have real ownership or incentive-option exposure in the new entity, signaling genuine commitment rather than a dumping ground? — askWhen: analyzing any spin-off situation, anchor: corporate-culture
  > "Insider participation is a key area. Are managers of the new spin-off incentives along the same lines as shareholders?" @line 10051
- **pattern**: What will management do with excess cash - buy back stock, pay dividends, or make acquisitions - and is that a good or bad use of capital at today's price? — askWhen: a company is sitting on cash above what its operations need, anchor: corporate-culture
  > "How will they redeploy the cash? Will they dissipate the cash or use it wisely like returning it to shareholders?" @line 254
- **pattern**: Are receivables and/or finished-goods inventory growing meaningfully faster than sales, signaling channel-stuffing or slowing real demand? — askWhen: reviewing balance-sheet quality of earnings, anchor: business-model

## Concepts
- **title**: Magic Formula (return on capital x earnings yield) — question: Does the company rank well on BOTH pre-tax return on tangible capital and pre-tax earnings yield versus the broad universe? test: rank by EBIT/(NWC+NFA) and separately by EBIT/EV, sum the two ranks, buy the best combined-rank names and hold about a year. evidence: a 17-year backtest averaged 30.8%/yr vs 12.3%/yr on the full universe and 22.9% vs 12.4% restricted to the largest 1,000 stocks, with returns falling off monotonically decile by decile.
  > "ranked stocks based on the best return on capital (1 - 3500) and earnings yield (1 3500) and then combined the rankings using an equal weighting." @line 4480
- **title**: Enterprise Value framework (EV/EBIT over P/E or P/S) — question: What would a buyer actually have to pay for the whole earnings stream, independent of how the company happens to be capitalized? test: EV = market cap + net interest-bearing debt - excess cash, divided by (normalized) EBIT, compared against a floor bond-yield hurdle. evidence: two identical-EBIT companies with different leverage produce very different, misleading P/E and P/S ratios while their EV/EBIT is identical.
  > "The different capitalization skews P/E and P/S but EV takes into account the different debt levels and lets you compare the true earnings stream that can be leveraged by a buyer or not." @line 13554
- **title**: Maintenance capex vs. growth capex (true owner earnings) — question: How much must the business spend just to keep current earnings power flat, versus how much is optional growth spending? test: separate capex into maintenance and growth and use EBIT roughly as EBITDA minus maintenance capex rather than EBITDA or reported net income. evidence: the hotel example where $25/yr of routine capex plus a five-year-amortized $400 refurbishment implies true maintenance capex of $105/yr, not $25.
  > "Maintenance capex what it would take to keep earnings the same amount in the year you are looking at." @line 10173
- **title**: Margin of safety via normalized, not trailing or projected, earnings — question: If the normalized-earnings estimate is off by a reasonable margin, does the position still avoid losing money at today's price? test: buy only at a large discount to a conservatively normalized multi-year-out earnings estimate, and pass entirely when normalized earnings cannot be estimated with confidence. evidence: repeated refusal throughout the course to price fashion retailers, biotech patent cliffs, or opaque conglomerates once normalized earnings could not be pinned down.
  > "If I am unable to normalize earnings, then pass on the opportunity or set aside." @line 8853
- **title**: Spin-off mispricing mechanics (forced/indifferent selling plus hidden insider incentive) — question: Is this spin-off small, complex, or unloved enough that institutions will dump it regardless of merit, while insiders have a real incentive to make it succeed? test: compare the spin-off's size to the total distribution, read the Form 10 for insider ownership and the timing of incentive-option strikes, and look for non-economic sellers such as index funds or mandate-restricted mutual funds. evidence: Host Marriott, Sears/Allstate/Dean Witter, and Liberty Media/TCI, in each of which the "toxic" or illiquid piece became the multi-bagger.
  > "A previously hidden investment opportunity is created or revealed. In the case of Host Marriot, there was tremendous leverage." @line 10055
- **title**: Quality-of-earnings divergence check (A/R and inventory vs. sales) — question: Are receivables and/or finished-goods inventory growing meaningfully faster than sales? test: compare year-over-year growth of sales, A/R, and the raw-materials/WIP-versus-finished-goods split of inventory; a "negative inventory divergence" where finished goods rise while WIP falls flags trouble ahead. evidence: Commodore International (A/R +34% vs sales +16.7%) and Sunbeam's 1997 bill-and-hold channel stuffing both preceded earnings blow-ups.
  > "The best method I have ever discovered to predict future downwards earnings revisions by Wall Street security analysts-is a careful analysis of A/R and inventories." @line 14751
- **title**: Kelly Criterion position sizing (edge/odds) — question: Given a genuine information or valuation edge, how much of the portfolio should a single bet carry without risking ruin on a bad run? test: bet size is roughly edge divided by odds, scaled down because a real-world edge is never as certain as a textbook one. evidence: the horse-racing example where true odds of 1-in-3 against posted odds of 5-to-1 imply betting 20% of bankroll, cited to justify concentrated 5-9-position portfolios and capped LEAP sizing.
  > "The formula says that the optimal wager size is determined according to the following fraction: Edge/Odds" @line 6137
- **title**: Circle of competence with a concentrated "punch card" portfolio — question: Is this inside an area understood well enough to estimate normalized earnings with confidence, and is it better than the worst current holding? test: limit the portfolio to roughly 5-9 positions (or "20 punches" over a lifetime), sized by conviction and margin of safety, rather than diversifying broadly. evidence: Gotham Capital's 5-8 position portfolio, the 20-hole punch-card exercise, and Linda Greenblatt's decade of high-20s returns from staying entirely within retail.
  > "recommend to you a 6 to 9 stock portfolio. It is a way to get rich actually." @line 5826

## Metrics
- **name**: Earnings Yield (EBIT/EV) — formula as stated: "Normalized" EBIT/Enterprise Value, i.e. pre-tax operating earnings divided by enterprise value; reading: compared against a minimum ~6% hurdle rate, never used lower even when Treasury yields sit below 6%; bench notes: the "cheap" half of the Magic Formula ranking.
  > "WHAT YOU PAY: "Normalized" EBIT/Enterprise Value (What I pay or pre-tax earnings yield)." @line 567
- **name**: Return on Invested Capital, ROIC (EBIT/(NWC+NFA)) — formula as stated: pre-tax operating income divided by net working capital plus net fixed assets, i.e. tangible capital excluding goodwill; reading: 20%+ pre-tax is attractive, businesses well under that after tax are candidate value traps; bench notes: the "good" half of the Magic Formula ranking.
  > "The first thing I look at ROIC = EBIT/ (NWC + Net Equipment). How good a business is this?" @line 324
- **name**: 6% minimum hurdle rate (bond-yield floor) — formula as stated: never use less than 6% as the comparison/discount rate even when the 10-year Treasury yields less; reading: equivalent to roughly a 16.66x P/E ceiling absent extra conviction to pay up for growth; bench notes: applied against every equity purchase discussed across the course.
  > "Never lower than 6% even if the rates are 4.5%. You know Buffett confirmed that when rates are below 6%, I use 6%." @line 688
- **name**: Free Cash Flow (owner earnings) — formula as stated: (Net Income + Depreciation + Amortization) minus CAP/EX; reading: represents cash available for dividends, buybacks, debt paydown, or acquisitions; bench notes: preferred over EBITDA, which is said to badly overstate cash flow once real capex is ignored.
  > "(Net Income + Depreciation + Amortization) minus CAP/EX Net income is accounting earnings after subtracting depreciation, amortization, interest and taxes." @line 13575
- **name**: Kelly bet size (Edge/Odds) — formula as stated: optimal wager equals edge divided by odds; reading: true odds of 1-in-3 versus posted odds of 5-to-1 imply betting 20% of bankroll; bench notes: cited as the rationale for concentrated sizing, never for a bet one is "absolutely, positively sure" of, which the text says almost never happens.
  > "So according to Kelly, the edge is 1 and the odds are 5. Plug in the numbers and you get 1/5. You should bet 20% of your bankroll." @line 6152
- **name**: Enterprise Value (EV) — formula as stated: market capitalization (price times shares outstanding) plus net interest-bearing debt; reading: subtract only "excess" cash not needed to run the business day to day, not the full cash balance; bench notes: the standard denominator beneath every earnings-yield calculation in the course.
  > "EV is market capitalization (Price times shares/out) + Net interest bearing debt (Debt includes the current portion)." @line 13494

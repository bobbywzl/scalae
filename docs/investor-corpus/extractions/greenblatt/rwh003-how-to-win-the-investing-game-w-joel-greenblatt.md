## Question patterns

## Concepts

- **title**: "Cheap AND good, not cheap OR good" (the Magic Formula thesis) — combining a crude quality screen (high return on tangible capital) with a crude value screen (earnings yield) and ranking by both, tested by decile from 1987 forward; the joint long-only ranking beat lower deciles consistently, but a long/short version of the same signal could still be wiped out in a single bad year (2000), so the edge must be held long and patient, not levered against its own volatility
  > "it turned out that the top decile beat the second decile, beat the third decile, beat the fourth decile." @line 284

- **title**: "Size positions by loss-risk, not by gain-potential" — position size should track how little you stand to lose if the thesis is wrong, not how much you could make if it is right; this is what let a well-understood special situation grow to an outsized share of the portfolio without feeling reckless
  > "I didn’t size my positions based on how much money I could make. I took bigger positions, you’re able to take bigger positions in things you can’t lose much money in." @line 95

- **title**: "The screw loose" (temperament as the scarce edge) — durable success in concentrated, contrarian investing requires a temperamental tolerance for pain that lets the investor recover psychologically within days rather than being paralyzed; framed as near-prerequisite, not a nice-to-have
  > "you have to have a little bit of a screw loose to take the pain, especially with a very concentrated portfolio that a number of people I know, pursue." @line 173

- **title**: "Owning businesses vs. owning quotes" — the identical concentrated allocation reads as prudent when framed as buying six or eight private businesses on a multi-year horizon, and as reckless when re-priced daily as volatile stock quotes; the underlying risk hasn't changed, only the marking and framing have, which is exactly what unmarked private-equity vehicles exploit to "smooth the ride"
  > "No one would say you’re crazy because you’re thinking of them as businesses. You divided your investment in its businesses." @line 140

- **title**: "Reverse-engineering a great investor's precedent purchase" — when deciding whether to pay up for quality, don't just admire a legendary buy from a distance; go back and reconstruct exactly what price/multiple was actually paid, adjust for structural differences (reinvestment rate, payout policy) between that business and the one being considered today, and compare the adjusted multiples directly
  > "went back to look at what he paid and made adjustments for the differences in the businesses, for instance Coca to reinvest some of its money to grow." @line 233

- **title**: "Manage skill against a safe-rate benchmark, not price volatility" — the right way to judge whether an investor did well is to compare the return achieved to the risk actually taken (measured against a safe/risk-free alternative), not to a broad-market benchmark or to how much the position's quoted price bounced around
  > "It has nothing to do with how much the stock bounces around that you bought. It really has to do with, what was the risk that you would lose?" @line 314

- **title**: "Circle of competence as a discipline, not a cage" — stay inside the boundary of what you actually understand and can value, while continuing to try to expand that boundary deliberately; the discipline is knowing where the boundary currently is, not refusing ever to move it
  > "understand what your circle of competence is. Doesn’t mean you can’t expand that circle of competence, but understand what it is and only play the game that you can play." @line 551

- **title**: "The 'No-Man' partnership (Munger/Rob Goldstein model)" — the highest-value investing partner is one whose agreement carries genuine informational weight because disagreement costs them nothing; an idea's weight comes from surviving an independent, adversarial re-derivation, not from being proposed by someone trusted
  > "that carries no weight with him until he’s done his work. Will not be a yes-man in any way, shape or form." @line 413

## Metrics

- **name**: Magic Formula quality screen — return on tangible capital — formula as stated: "high returns on tangible capital," a deliberately crude proxy for business quality drawn from reading Buffett's letters; reading: higher = better business, used to rank stocks into deciles jointly with the value screen; bench notes: explicitly not a refined multi-factor score — chosen to be simple enough to compute across the whole market
  > "So crude metric for good was high returns on tangible capital if you read through Buffet’s letters, that’s really what he’s looking for." @line 281

- **name**: Magic Formula value screen — earnings yield vs. safe-rate benchmark — formula as stated: compare the yield from owning the business's earnings (e.g. "10% yield") against a safe alternative like a bank deposit (e.g. "5% in a bank"); reading: the wider the spread over the safe rate — especially with expected growth — the more attractive the bet; bench notes: this is the "cheap" half of the Magic Formula, combined by decile with the quality screen above, not blended by a stated numeric weight
  > "if you could get 5% in a bank and you could get 10% yield by owning the earnings of this business, and you thought it was going to grow, that sounds better." @line 281

- **name**: Forward two-year return estimate, S&P 500 vs. Gotham's deep-value "Value 1000" portfolio (as stated in this March 2022 interview) — formula as stated: apply the same 30-year historical valuation-based model to the S&P 500's then-current pricing and to the ~600-700 stock cheap-weighted Value 1000 portfolio; reading: model implied mid-single-digit returns for the S&P 500 over the next two years vs. roughly 35%-40% for the value portfolio over the same period; bench notes: explicitly a backward-fit historical model, not a guarantee ("Doesn't mean that'll happen")
  > "it says mid-single digit returns over the next two years. Whereas that value portfolio using the same analysis says, I think it’s going to earn 35% to 40% over the next couple of years, based on past history." @line 323

- **name**: Career-long annualized return (Gotham Capital, 1985-1994ish, concentrated era) — formula as stated: "Joel famously set up a hedge fund when he was 27 years old and then managed to average a return of 40% a year over the next 20 years" (interviewer's framing, confirmed without correction by Greenblatt in the conversation) — reading: at 40%/year, "$1 million grows into $836 million"; bench notes: earned via a concentrated (six-to-eight-position) special-situations portfolio sized by downside risk, not upside potential — the track record underlying every later strategy shift discussed in the interview
  > "Greenblatt famously averaged 40% a year over 20 years. At that rate, $1 million grows into $836 million." @line 63

## Other
- **origin story — dropping out of law school into a bear-market Wall Street job**: Greenblatt reveals that his first full-time Wall Street job — risk arbitrage — began right after he dropped out of law school, and landed at "the tail end of a bear market" when the outcome felt uncertain; he frames the security he found came not from early success but from realizing this was the field he'd be good at regardless of that rocky start.
  > "I would say when I took my first full-time job on Wall Street, after dropping out a law school, it didn’t go particularly well." @line 542
- **memorable framing — "buying dollar bills for 40 cents" analog (Host Marriott spinoff)**: Greenblatt's signature 40%-of-assets bet on the Host Marriott spinoff is his own real-world version of the classic value-investing image: paying $4 for assets he calculated were worth $6, unencumbered by debt, plus an entire additional debt-laden subsidiary business thrown in essentially for free.
  > "So actually, I paid $4 for what I thought was $6 worth of assets that were unencumbered by debt." @line 122
- **distinctive teaching anecdote — the Florida Cypress Gardens sinkhole**: One of Greenblatt's most memorable disaster stories — a merger-arbitrage bet in the very first month he went out on his own, undone when the target company's amusement park pavilions literally collapsed into a sinkhole, a risk "not really your fault" and impossible to have checklisted for.
  > "Florida Cypress Gardens fell into what’s called a sinkhole, meaning the main pavilions of Florida Cypress Gardens literally fell into a hole that appeared out of nowhere." @line 140
- **track-record color — the 140%-up, then -17%-for-family-money whipsaw**: In Gotham's first 15 months (from April 1985) the fund was up 140%; flush with that success, Greenblatt called in siblings and family to invest — only to lose 17% of their money in the following six months, which he singles out as one of the hardest experiences of his career precisely because it was other people's trust, not his own capital, on the line.
  > "First 15 months, we were up 140%. There was no worry that Mike Milken was going to come and yell at me for that." @line 173
- **personal history — meeting Buffett in person for the first time**: Despite decades of studying Buffett from afar and reverse-engineering his Coca-Cola purchase to price Moody's, Greenblatt didn't meet Buffett until bringing his Columbia class to Omaha; he describes himself as unexpectedly "incredibly shy" in the moment, and the encounter's lasting lesson was not investing technique but Buffett's humility and graciousness at that level of success.
  > "he handed me his fat wallet, and I got a good picture with him. I would just say that I was stunned at how gracious he was." @line 269

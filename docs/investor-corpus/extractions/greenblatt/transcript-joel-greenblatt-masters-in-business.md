## Question patterns

- **pattern**: How well does this business convert the working capital and fixed assets already tied up in it into earnings — i.e., what is its return on tangible capital? — askWhen: judging whether a statistically cheap business is also a "good" business, before ranking it for purchase, anchor: business-model
  > "every business needs working capital, every business needs fixed assets, how well does it convert its working capital and fixed assets into earnings" @line 424

- **pattern**: How does this business's cheapness compare to similar companies, to the market as a whole, and to its own history — not just to its book value or sales? — askWhen: sizing whether a price is genuinely cheap using an owner's free-cash-flow yield, rather than a bare low price-to-book or low price-to-sales screen, anchor: business-model
  > "how cheap is this business relative to similar companies, how cheap is it relative to all companies, how cheap is it relative to history" @line 422

- **pattern**: Is management running this business the way an owner who bought the whole thing outright would — good prospects, run well, shareholders treated well — or just running up a stock price? — askWhen: reading whether a business is genuinely "good," not just statistically cheap, before sizing a position meant to be held through a period of looking wrong, anchor: culture
  > "if you find businesses where the managements really good, the prospects for the business are good, it’s run well, they treat shareholders well" @line 128

- **pattern**: Am I trading on a genuine change in what the underlying business is worth, or on the stock price bouncing around like an emotionally-priced quote? — askWhen: deciding whether to act on a price move, especially when the position has moved against the stated thesis, anchor: business-model
  > "stocks are actually ownership shares of business that you value and try to buy at a discount, they’re not pieces of paper the bounce around" @line 78

- **pattern**: Is this security priced on current, checkable earnings, or on a specific story about earnings several years out (a "hope stock")? — askWhen: evaluating whether a richly-priced grower deserves a long position or a short, anchor: business-model
  > "the stocks were short at stock trading at 40, 50, 100 times earnings, these are hope stocks" @line 250

- **pattern**: If I stripped away every day's stock quote and the market's emotional noise, and just asked what a private buyer of the whole business would pay, would this price still look wrong? — askWhen: teaching or self-checking discipline against short-term market noise, anchor: business-model

## Concepts

- **title**: Stocks are ownership shares of businesses, not bouncing pieces of paper — question: am I reacting to a price quote or chart, or to a genuine change in what the underlying business is worth?; test: strip away daily quotes and portfolio-theory statistics (Sharpe/Sortino ratios, volatility); ask only what a private buyer of the whole business would pay for it and why
  > "stocks are actually ownership shares of business that you value and try to buy at a discount" @line 78

- **title**: Patience is the edge — the market will agree with you, just not on a schedule you control — question: have I done valuation work good enough to hold through an unknown (but not indefinite) period of looking wrong, or am I relying on the market to confirm me quickly?; test: treat convergence of price to value as a "when," not an "if" — a stagnant or falling price alone is not disproof of a thesis built on good work
  > "I would say probably the secret to being successful is patience" @line 556
  > "It could be a couple of weeks, it could be two or three years, but if they do good work, the market will agree with them" @line 558

- **title**: Diversify across many "right on average" bets once the process can systematically value hundreds of businesses — question: is my edge in this strategy about being right on every position, or about being right on average across a sample large enough that one bad outcome cannot wreck the result?; test: where a repeatable, checkable valuation process exists at scale, prefer owning hundreds of names (long the cheap/good, short the expensive/hope) over concentrating in a handful, because aberrationally-bad-outcome risk falls as the sample grows — the same reason insurers do not underwrite five lives
  > "insurance companies don’t insure five people because even if you do great underwriting of those five people someone steps off a curb" @line 484

- **title**: Spinoffs and other unwanted-security corners are "right for mispricing" — question: is this security in a structurally overlooked corner of the market (given away rather than sold to willing buyers), regardless of whether the average outcome in that category is good or bad?; test: don't predict which one will be the next big winner — instead look for a specific comparable transaction that shows the current price is out of line, the way antique-hunters find an underpriced piece by pointing to what a similar piece already sold for
  > "a similar pain just went for three times as much, that’s a lot easier to do than to project or predict the next Picasso" @line 342

- **title**: Operating leverage as a double-edged multiplier — question: how much of this business's incremental revenue drops straight to the bottom line, and have I stress-tested the downside with the same seriousness as the upside?; test: identify fixed-cost capacity structures (e.g. convention floor space, real estate, plant capacity) where a small revenue shortfall causes an outsized earnings collapse, symmetric to the outsized earnings gain on the upside
  > "I learned a lesson in operating leverage where when you don’t get that $62 and it only cost you $2, $60 dollars of less earnings drop straight to the bottom line as well" @line 538

- **title**: The jellybean-jar experiment — private judgment vs. socially-anchored guessing — question: is my valuation an independent, disciplined estimate, or has it been contaminated by seeing/hearing what everyone else around me thinks?; test: form a private, first-guess estimate (like the jar's actual bean count) before being exposed to the crowd's running commentary (like the public second-guess), because the public guess systematically drifts from the disciplined private one
  > "the average for the 3 x 5 cards you know the first guess was 1771 jellybeans, that’s what it average to, and there actually 1776, jellybeans in the jar" @line 386

## Metrics

- **name**: Return on tangible capital (Greenblatt) — formula: pretax operating earnings divided by the tangible capital (working capital + fixed assets) the business needs to generate them, stated via his worked example — a $400,000 store earning $200,000/yr is a 50% return on tangible capital, versus a "Just Broccoli" store on the same $400,000 build-out earning only $10,000/yr (2.5%); reading: the "good" half of the Magic Formula — all else equal, prefer the business that converts more of its tangible capital into earnings; benchNotes: pretax and earnings-based (never EBITDA), measured against genuine invested tangible capital, not total assets or book equity broadly — anchor: business-model
  > "he is looking for businesses that earn high returns on tangible capital" @line 150
  > "50 percent return on tangible capital, maybe I’ll open some more stores" @line 152

- **name**: Earnings yield — EBIT to Enterprise Value (Greenblatt) — formula: earnings before interest and taxes divided by enterprise value, described as the "rent" a purchase price would yield, like a house's rental income relative to its price; reading: the "cheap" half of the Magic Formula, ranked across companies and combined with the return-on-tangible-capital rank — the combined top decile outperformed each lower decile in order in his tested period; benchNotes: EBIT (never EBITDA) over enterprise value (equity + net debt) — must be paired with the "good" metric above so cheapness is never read alone — anchor: business-model
  > "we really rank companies based on a simple metric that was earnings before interest and taxes to enterprise value" @line 404

- **name**: S&P 500 bottoms-up valuation percentile (28-year lookback) — formula: value every stock in the S&P 500 (and the top ~3000 companies) bottoms-up every trading day, and rank the current aggregate valuation against its own 28-year history of daily readings; reading: at the time of the interview the market stood in the "16th percentile towards expensive," i.e. cheaper 84% of the time over 28 years, with two-year forward returns from that percentile historically averaging 8-10%, well under the 28-year 9-10% average annual baseline; benchNotes: not a market-timing prediction — Greenblatt is explicit it describes what has happened from this valuation level historically, not what will happen — anchor: business-model
  > "right now we stand in the 16th percentile towards expensive over those 28 years. Meaning the market’s been cheaper 84 percent of the time for the S&P" @line 282

- **name**: Magic Formula backtested return spread (1988-2004, small caps; Russell 1000, large caps) — formula: rank companies by combined decile of the cheap and good metrics above, form a long-only portfolio of the top combined decile, measured against the broad benchmark over the same period; reading: ~30%/year vs. ~12%/year for the small-cap universe 1988-2004, and ~22%/year vs. ~12%/year for the Russell 1000 (large-cap) version of the same test; benchNotes: long-only, not the long/short decile-1-vs-decile-10 pairing that failed in 2000 elsewhere in the corpus — anchor: business-model
  > "from 1988 to 2004 this portfolio would’ve returned about 30 percent a year while at the same time the market would’ve given you a little over 12 percent" @line 434

## Other
- **personal history — negotiating with Michael Milken while he kept Ronald Perelman waiting**: Greenblatt's origin-capital story is a memorable piece of Wall Street color: as an unproven 27-year-old with a "pretty rare" bottom line for himself, he refused to budge on his terms in a room with Milken — who was negotiating "like this was the biggest deal of his life" even while a much larger Perelman/Revlon deal waited — and ultimately got his terms from Milken's brother.
  > "And so I wouldn’t give in and we sat in there for an hour while he kept Ronald Perelman waiting because he was about to buy Revlon" @line 94
- **memorable framing — the "Just Broccoli" store analogy for return on tangible capital**: Greenblatt's signature teaching device for "good" businesses, built for his own kids' understanding, compares a $400,000 store earning $200,000/year (50% return on tangible capital) to an identically-priced "Just Broccoli" store earning only $10,000/year (2.5%) — a plainly bad idea that still costs the same to build, making the quality gap concrete rather than abstract.
  > "if all that cost you $400,000 and the store earns you $200,000 a year, that’s a 50 percent return on tangible capital" @line 426
- **distinctive teaching anecdote — the jellybean jar and Harlem ninth-graders**: After "crashing and burning" trying to explain markets to a room of orthopedic surgeons, Greenblatt found the same lesson landed with ninth-graders from Harlem by having them privately guess a jellybean jar's count (average: 1,771, actual: 1,776 — remarkably accurate) versus guessing aloud one by one after hearing others (average dropped to 850) — his live demonstration that the "second guess" is what the stock market actually is.
  > "and I explained to the kids that the second guess was actually the stock market" @line 394
- **track-record color — the "not trying very hard method" backtest that became The Little Book**: Gotham's very first test of combining crude cheap and good metrics — not one of thousands of computer runs, but literally the first test run — worked so well it directly became The Little Book That Beats the Market, prompting Greenblatt and Rob Goldstein to look at each other and ask whether their actual business-valuation skill could improve on a method that worked well "without trying."
  > "It’s not like we spun the computer thousands of times, this was the very first test we did and it came out so phenomenally well that I wrote a book about it" @line 166
- **personal history — the accidental title "You Can Be a Stock Market Genius"**: Greenblatt reveals the book's now-famous title was a last-minute accident: the intended title, "Any Fool Can Be a Stock Market Genius," had to be scrapped within 24 hours because the Motley Fools published a competing book, and his father suggested the substitute over a family conversation.
  > "it was really supposed to be Any Fool Can Be A Stock Market Genius, but the Motley Fools came out with a book and so I had about 24 hours to change the name" @line 308

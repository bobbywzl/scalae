## Concepts

- **title**: Conventional vs. Penetrating Approaches to Security Analysis — conventional relies on identifying quality and prospects; penetrating relies on intrinsic value and margin of safety; test: when does each method succeed vs fail; evidence: conventional works well for quality stocks at fair prices but struggles with growth stock timing and near-term forecasting, penetrating requires both market timing discipline and security selection skill
  > "The first I call the conventional one, and that is based primarily on quality and on prospects. The second I call, in complimentary fashion, the penetrating one, and that is based upon value." @line 101-104

- **title**: The Analyst's Proper Role and Required Qualifications — analyst should be wise (technically competent, experienced, prudent) not primarily shrewd; test: does the analyst's work output communicate defensible valuation logic; evidence: stating fair value ranges, identifying market premiums for growth, confirming market hasn't already discounted the catalyst
  > "I might say rather that a security analyst should be required to be wise, in the sense that he is technically competent, that he is experienced, and that he is prudent." @line 170-172

- **title**: The Unpopularity Trap in Value Investing — undervalued securities are unpopular for a reason; when general market declines, unpopular stocks decline sharply too, negating the value opportunity; test: does the unpopular security hold up during market corrections; evidence: lower-priced undervalued stocks show higher percentage losses than blue chips in recessions
  > "If Mandel or some similar company sells at less than your idea of value, it sells so because it is not popular; and it is not going to get more popular during periods when the market as a whole is declining considerably." @line 289-292

- **title**: Right Price for Right Stock — buying good stocks at fair prices is the surest path to consistent results; mistakes come from overpaying for quality or buying poor stocks regardless of price; test: is the security trading within a defensible valuation range; evidence: investment counsel firms survive by adhering to this discipline
  > "Investors do not make mistakes, or bad mistakes, in buying good stocks at fair prices. They make their serious mistakes by buying poor stocks, particularly the ones that are pushed for various reasons." @line 132-134

- **title**: Skill/luck-dependence of growth-stock selection vs. teachable "wisdom" — question: Is successful growth-stock selection a communicable, teachable technique, or does it fundamentally depend on shrewdness/luck that cannot be systematized into a canon of security analysis?, test: Attempt to codify a repeatable growth-stock-selection method and test whether a typical trained analyst (not an exceptional one) can apply it successfully; compare results against the wise-but-not-shrewd conventional/value approaches, evidence: Graham states growth-stock success "comes from being smart or shrewd" and explicitly doubts "whether you can establish a technique of a communicable sort... by which you will be enabled to identify" winning growth stocks — contrasting this with wisdom (competence, experience, prudence), which he considers teachable.
  > "It is quite questionable to my mind whether you can establish a technique of a communicable sort -- that a good instructor can pass on to his pupil -- by which you will be enabled to identify those stocks not only which have good prospects of growth but which have not already discounted pretty much those prospects in the market." @line 161-164

- **title**: Popularity discount does not protect a bargain stock in a general decline — question: Does an undervalued stock's low price provide extra downside protection during a broad market decline, or does its unpopularity make it equally or more vulnerable? — test: Compare the percentage price decline of low-priced/unpopular bargain stocks against blue-chip stocks during major market corrections, evidence: Graham states the undervalued security "will go down about as fast" as blue chips in percentage terms during a decline, and that lower-priced securities historically lose more percentagewise in important recessions than higher-priced ones — directly countering the intuition that cheapness itself is a defense.
  > "In terms of percentage I would say yes, on the whole. It will go down about as fast, because the undervalued security tends to be a lower-priced security" @line 297-299

## Metrics

- **name**: Earnings trend continuation probability — formula: review 1926-1930 performance vs 1936-onwards performance for companies showing improvement or decline; test whether the trend continues or reverses; finding: at least as many reversals as continuations; reading: trends are unreliable guides to future performance; bench notes: Collins' method assumes continuity (conservative bias) while Graham's method explicitly discounts for reversion risk; source priority: actual earnings history across full business cycles
  > "We made a rather intensive study on the subject of whether earnings trends did or did not continue. We tried to find out what happened to companies showing an improvement in their earnings from 1926 to 1930, comparing them further with 1936???; and also those that had failed to show improvement in the period." @line 79-82

- **name**: Post-war earnings projection method (Collins approach) — formula: (1) industry sales projections adjusted to $112B national income (2) company-specific adjustments based on 1929-1940 trend performance vs industry average (3) net before taxes based on pre-war ratios (4) 40% tax rate; range: $15.96 to $17.58 per Dow unit; reading: represents "active post-war year" earnings, more optimistic than normalized average; bench notes: differs from Graham's estimate partly due to different national income assumption (Collins $112B vs actual 1946 at $165B) and tax rate assumptions
  > "He starts from industry sales projections which have been made by the Committee for Economic Development of the Department of Commerce, and he adjusts them to an expected national income of $112-billion." @line 47-49

- **name**: Market valuation assessment framework — formula: (1) identify if market price is low relative to past pattern (2) compute through simple valuation methods (3) distinguish between change in market sentiment vs change in underlying fundamentals; reading: analyst should maintain stable view of future earnings unless convinced of significant underlying change; bench notes: mechanical systems (Yale method) work but create long waits; timing appears consistently profitable at low market levels only
  > "The analyst identifies low market levels in relation to the past pattern of the market and by simple valuation methods such as those that we have been discussing. And bear in mind that the good analyst doesn't change his concept of what the earnings of the next five years are going to be just because the market happens to be pessimistic at one time, or optimistic at another." @line 254-258

- **name**: Yale-method mechanical rebalancing rule — formula: Sell a fixed percentage of stock holdings as prices rise above a median/average level; convert a fixed percentage of bonds into stocks as prices fall below that level, reading: A disciplined, rules-based counter-cyclical rebalancing system that "stands up in the light of experience," but its major drawback is the long wait between profitable rebalancing triggers — a practical problem for a salaried analyst who must justify continuous activity, benchNotes: Not a stock-selection tool but a market-level timing overlay; complements (does not replace) individual-security valuation work.
  > "Now he can also follow a mechanical system of operating in the market, if he wishes, like the Yale University method that many of you are familiar with. In this you sell a certain percentage of your stocks as they go up, or you convert a certain percentage of your bonds into stocks as they go down, from some median or average level." @line 261-264

## Other

- **Graham's opening classroom banter after a month's hiatus**: The lecture opens with Graham's characteristic dry, self-deprecating humor — welcoming students back from "a month's rest" and inviting them to be "ready to absorb some more punishment" — a small but vivid data point on his classroom persona as warm, witty, and self-aware about the density of his material.
  > "You have all had a month's rest since the last lecture. I hope you had a pleasant vacation during that period and you are now ready to absorb some more punishment." @line 16-18

- **Graham explicitly flags his own commentary as personal opinion, not doctrine**: Before critiquing the five lines of conventional and penetrating analysis, Graham is careful to frame his assessment as "basically, a personal opinion" rather than "the standard view of the work of the security analyst" — a notable moment of epistemic humility distinguishing his own judgment from an authoritative canon, even though he was arguably the field's leading authority.
  > "Of course, I am expressing, basically, a personal opinion, which is derived from experience and observation and a great deal of thought; but it should not be taken as in any sense representing the standard view of the work of the security analyst." @line 124-126

- **A concrete illustration of how the same "next year will be better" logic fails**: Graham names two actual 1946-47 companies — U.S. Plywood at 34 and National Department Stores at 17 — with opposite near-term outlooks, and shows the logic that "buy the one whose year will be better" doesn't follow, because the current prices may already discount years of divergent earnings; a vivid worked example rather than an abstraction.
  > "the price of 34 for U.S. Plywood might have discounted very good earnings for three years, and the price of National Department Stores might theoretically have discounted poor earnings for three years" @line 208-210

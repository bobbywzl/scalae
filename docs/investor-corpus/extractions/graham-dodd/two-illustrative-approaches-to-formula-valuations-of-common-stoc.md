## Concepts

- **title**: Backward-Looking Analysis as Incomplete Foundation for Forward Valuation — test: does past performance correlation to future returns hold across different market regimes?, evidence: past record provides data but not certainty; analyst judgment must bridge gap
  > "the relationship between past and future proves significant enough to justify the analyst's preoccupation with the statistical record" @line 54-55

- **title**: Built-in Instability in Growth-Stock Valuations — test: do high-growth stocks show greater valuation volatility across market cycles?, evidence: no a priori rule limits forward-looking period; investor mood (bull vs. bear) drives horizon assumption
  > "in bull markets investors and analysts will tend to see far and hopefully ahead, whereas at other times they will not" @line 30-32

- **title**: Quality Elements as Bounded Multipliers — test: can a company's profitability, growth, stability, and payout ratio be reduced to a single quality index?, evidence: equal weighting of four factors provides composite quality score applicable as earnings multiplier
  > "of the company as against the over-all quality of the group" @line 239-240

- **title**: Asset Value as Long-Run Insurance Despite Current Market Indifference — test: do asset values eventually exert pricing pressure through M&A, management change, or competitive repositioning?, evidence: legal valuations (tax/merger cases) consistently weight assets; current market ignores but future may reassert
  > "But it may have some long-run effect on future market price, and thus it has a claim to be considered seri-ously" @line 303-304

- **title**: Analyst's Independent Judgment as the True Deliverable — question: does the analyst's process end at calculating a number, or does it culminate in an independent judgment ready to contest the market's verdict?, test: the process must produce a stance the analyst is willing to hold against daily market opinion, not merely a restated market consensus, evidence: Graham frames formula valuation as a "point of departure" for the analyst's own exploration, not a final verdict
  > "They may prefer to exercise an independent judgment—one not controlled by the daily verdict of the market place, but ready at times to take definite issue with it" @line 641-644

- **title**: Symmetric Discrepancy Diagnosis (Overvaluation and Undervaluation as Twin Signals) — question: when formula value and price disagree, is the size of disagreement roughly balanced between overpriced and underpriced names, or skewed?, test: count and compare the number/magnitude of stocks trading at premiums vs. discounts to formula value within the same universe, evidence: Graham reports named extremes at both ends — Westinghouse at a 100% premium, United Aircraft at a 50% discount — treating the symmetric split as diagnostic of the method's calibration
  > "At the extremes we find Westinghouse selling at a 100% "premium," and United Aircraft at about a 50% "discount."" @line 386-388

- **title**: Growth Premium Traceable to Named Causes vs. Unexplained Divergence — question: can the market's growth expectation gap from a company's past record be traced to an identifiable structural reason?, test: distinguish cases with clear causal stories (defense-customer concentration, industry tailwind) from cases where the price tag is simply unreasonable, evidence: Graham explicitly separates clear-cut divergences from ones "not so easy to understand or to accept"
  > "The market may be right in its general feeling about a company's future, but the price tag it sets on that future may be quite unreasonable in either direction" @line 635-637

- **title**: The Squared Effect of Growth Expectations on Price — question: does the market double-count growth optimism—once in the higher earnings estimate, again in the higher multiplier applied to those earnings?, test: model price as (expected earnings) × (multiplier that itself rises with expected growth), an implicit squaring of the growth variable, evidence: Graham's 8G² formula makes growth expectation enter price twice, structurally
  > "an issue for which more than average growth is expected will have this fact shown to a double degree, or "squared," in its market price—first in the higher figure taken for future earnings, and second in the higher multiplier applied to those higher earnings" @line 438-441

## Metrics

- **name**: Quality Index from Composite of Four Earnings Factors — formula: (Profitability + Growth + Stability + Payout) / 4, where each component is indexed to Dow-Jones Average = 100, reading: Allied Chemical's index = 84% means its multiplier should be 16% below group multiplier
  > "of the company as against the over-all quality of the group" @line 239-240

- **name**: Profitability as Return on Invested Capital — formula: 1956 earnings / 1956 net assets, bench notes: Dow-Jones baseline 13.0%; measure of productive efficiency; stronger predictor of price-earnings ratio than past growth alone
  > "The rate of earnings on invested capital is perhaps the most logical measure of the success and quality of an enterprise" @line 242-243

- **name**: Growth as Multi-Period Per-Share Earnings Comparison — formula: (1947-56 average vs. 1947 actual) + (1956 vs. 1947-56 average); weak correlation to current P/E despite market focus, bench notes: Dow growth = 56%; examples show 31% past growth priced at 47x while 93% growth at 9.1x
  > "the expected future growth is in fact the major influence upon Current price-earnings ratios" @line 258-259

- **name**: Stability as Percentage Earnings Retained in Crisis — formula: earnings in worst shrinkage year / earnings in preceding peak (1937-38 and 1947-56 periods), bench notes: companies with high multipliers show greater than average stability
  > "Stability—as measured by the greatest shrinkage of profits in the periods 1937-1938 and 1947-1956" @line 220-221

- **name**: Dividend Pay-Out Ratio as Investor Expectation Signal — formula: 1956 dividends / 1956 earnings (or 1947-56 average if current below average), bench notes: Dow ratio = 64.3%; proxy for earning quality perception; equal weight to growth/profitability/stability
  > "Pay-out—as measured by the ratio of 1956 dividends to 1956 earnings" @line 226-227

- **name**: Implied Future Growth from Market Price (8G² Formula) — formula: Price = 8G² × E (where E = 1947-56 average; G = expected growth 1957-66 vs. 1947-56); solving G = √(Price / 8E), reading: Dow price 500 implies G = 1.5 (50% growth); price 400 implies G = 1.30 (30% growth)
  > "Price equals (E X G) X (8 X G), or 8G? x E, where E is the per-share earnings for 1947-56" @line 457-458

- **name**: Group Formula Value from Net Assets Plus Quality-Weighted Earnings Multiple — formula: Value = 1/5 × Net Assets + Quality Factor × base multiplier × 1947-56 average earnings, reading: at the 400-Dow-base anchor, the base multiplier is 12.5x; the same computation is re-run at a 500-Dow-base anchor with a 16.2x multiplier to bound the sensitivity of the resulting "valuations", benchNotes: the asset term is a fixed one-fifth-weight overlay on top of an earnings-multiple core — asset value never drives the number alone
  > ""Value" equals 1/5 Net Assets plus 12.5 x 1947-56 earnings or 55 plus 12.5 x 27.50 or 400" @line 353-355

- **name**: Dual Group-Level Anchors for Central Value — formula: two independently derived base values (400 = multi-method historical "Central Value" for the Dow; 500 = trailing twelve-month average market level) each seed a full parallel run of the individual-issue formula, reading: running the cross-sectional method against both bases brackets how sensitive individual "valuations" are to which market regime is used as the group anchor, benchNotes: guards against a single-anchor formula silently encoding one period's optimism or pessimism
  > "The 400 figure represented the approximate "Central Value" of the Dow-Jones Average, as found separately by a whole series of formula methods derived from historical relationships" @line 124-127

- **name**: Base No-Growth Multiplier, Judgment-Calibrated Not Empirically Fitted — formula: solve backward from an assumed reasonable group-level G (here 1.5, i.e., 50% decade growth) to fix the "zero-growth" base multiplier (8x) that then applies uniformly to every individual issue's G calculation, reading: the base multiplier is explicitly reverse-engineered from a judgment call about what looked reasonable at the 500 Dow level, not derived from an independent statistical fit, benchNotes: exposes the formula's hidden judgment input — a reader who disagrees with the 500-level "reasonable" call should re-derive a different base multiplier before trusting any individual G
  > "In fact he started with this estimate and worked back from it to get the basic multiplier of 8 to be applied to issues with no expected growth" @line 470-472

## Other

- **Durand's Petersburg-Paradox critique cited in a footnote**: Graham points readers outside his own quantitative apparatus to a formal academic critique questioning whether the growth-stock valuation problem is solvable at all — a rare moment of him flagging the limits of his own method rather than defending it.
  > "that the growth-stock problem offers no great hope of a satisfactory solution" @line 66-68

- **The closing line reframes the entire article's purpose**: after two full pages of formulas and tables, Graham ends not by asserting the formulas are right, but by calling them raw material for the analyst's own independent exploration — the humility underneath his whole quantitative apparatus.
  > "They give a concrete and elaborated picture of the past record, which the analyst may use as a point of departure for his individual exploration and discoveries in the field of investment values" @line 646-649

- **Naming the extremes by name, not by category**: rather than an abstract methodological claim, Graham commits to a falsifiable, specific prediction — identifying Westinghouse and United Aircraft by name as the Dow's two most extreme formula-vs-market discrepancies in 1957.
  > "At the extremes we find Westinghouse selling at a 100% "premium," and United Aircraft at about a 50% "discount."" @line 386-388

## Other
- **The White Motors thought experiment**: Graham opens the entire article as a personal, direct-address parable — casting the reader as the owner of a manufacturing business with a healthy balance sheet, then springing the reveal that real shareholders of White Motors actually sold at that absurd price. It is Graham's signature rhetorical device: converting an abstract balance-sheet argument into a gut-check personal decision.
  > "Suppose you were the owner of a large manufacturing business. Like many others, you lost money in 1931; the immediate prospects are not encouraging; you feel pessimistic and willing to sell out--cheap." @lines 12-13
- **The waggish barber's sign**: Graham reaches for an old joke — a barber's sign promising "we shave you for nothing and give you a drink" — as his closing image for the absurdity of stockholders handing over inventories and receivables for less than nothing while throwing in real estate and machinery as a bonus. A folk-humor anecdote used to land a serious point about mispricing.
  > "The waggish barber of the legend painted on his sign: What, do you think -We shave you for nothing and give you a drink! That, without the saving comma, might well be blazoned as the motto of the stock seller of to-day" @lines 161-163
- **The Columbia University study, 600 companies**: A biographical/institutional anchor: this June 1932 Forbes piece is grounded in original empirical research Graham personally directed at Columbia University's School of Business, surveying roughly 600 NYSE-listed industrials — the same period in which he was developing the material that would become Security Analysis (1934) with David Dodd.
  > "A study made at the Columbia University School of Business under the writer's direction, covering some 600 industrial companies listed on the New York Stock Exchange, disclosed that over 200 of them--or fully one out of three--have been selling at less than their net quick assets." @lines 47-49
- **The New York Stock Exchange president's own words on panic pricing**: Graham cites, seemingly approvingly as a fair diagnosis of the moment, a remark attributed to the sitting president of the NYSE about frightened people giving securities away — a contemporaneous quotation-within-the-quotation that captures how even Wall Street's own leadership described the 1932 bottom.
  > "As the president of the New York Stock Exchange testified, "in times like these frightened people give the United States of ours away."" @lines 60-62

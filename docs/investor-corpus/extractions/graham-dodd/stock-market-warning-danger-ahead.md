## Concepts

- **title**: Bull-Bear Market Cycle — test: Do extended bull markets inevitably reverse? — evidence: historical pattern of boom followed by systematic crash
  > "the stock market will continue to be essentially what it always was in the past—a place where a big bull market is inevitably followed by a big bear market," @line 838-841

- **title**: New Era Fallacy — test: Does belief conditions have fundamentally changed justify higher valuations? — evidence: 1920s and 1960s both claimed new conditions before crashes
  > "Then, as now, nearly everyone was convinced that we had entered a "New Era" of continued and dynamic prosperity which made all past market experience worse than useless," @line 504-507

- **title**: Speculation-Investment Semantic Confusion — test: Can speculators identify themselves as such? — evidence: public self-identification as investors while engaged in speculation
  > "Everyone now calls himself an investor, including a huge horde of speculators," @line 46-47

- **title**: Valuation Ratio Extremes as Warning Signal — test: When price-to-earnings reaches historical peaks, does correction follow? — evidence: 1959 ratios equal 1929, 1937, 1946 peaks
  > "and the ratio of price to earnings of the past 12 months was about 19 times." @line 285-286

- **title**: Speculative Excess in New Securities — test: Do excessive IPO valuations precede market crashes? — evidence: IPO spikes and high valuations mirror 1929 pattern
  > "There is no such thing as a free lunch." @line 776

- **title**: Proportional-decline law linking rally size to subsequent drawdown — question: rather than treating "average bear-market decline" as a flat historical constant, does past experience show that the SIZE of a decline scales with how large the preceding advance was, giving a more calibrated (and often scarier) forecast for very large rallies?, test: split historical advances into the largest-magnitude group and the smaller group, and compare each group's average subsequent decline., evidence: Graham's own data split (6 largest advances → 46% avg decline; 6 smaller advances → 37% avg decline).
  > "The record shows that declines have tended to be roughly proportional iv the previous ad- vanced, Thus, the 6 largest advances aver- aging 63 percent of the high level reached were followed by declines averaging 46 per- cent while the other 6 advances averaging 88 percent of the high produced declines averaging 37 percent." @line 158-165

- **title**: Prices determine value standards, not the reverse (advisor-behavior critique) — question: do professional forecasters and valuation services actually anchor their published "fair value" conclusions to fundamentals, or do their conclusions quietly track whatever direction the market has already moved?, test: contrast the same service's (or comparable services') published reaction to a record-LOW P/E in 1949 against their reaction to a record-HIGH P/E in 1959 — do they show symmetrical skepticism, or does bullish framing appear specifically at the high and bearish framing specifically at the low?, evidence: Graham's direct juxtaposition of one service's weak, equivocal bearishness at the 1949 low against another's weak, equivocal bullishness at the 1959 high.
  > "All my experience goes to show that most investment advisers take their opinions and measures of stock values from stock prices. In the stock market, value standards do not determine prices; prices determine value standards," @line 754-759

- **title**: "No such thing as a free lunch" as the governing law behind speculative excess — question: can inexperienced participants profit quickly in a rising market without eventually paying a corresponding, often larger, penalty?, test: track whether early "easy money" participants (in new-issue speculation, high-multiple new companies, artificially split-price securities) tend to hold their gains through a full cycle or give them back., evidence: Graham's explicit naming of electronics-stock speculation, 25x-earnings new issues, and the Studebaker-Packard price discrepancies as contemporary examples of the same 1929-style excess.

- **The corner-saloon "free lunch" origin story**: Graham explicitly glosses his own aphorism for a younger audience — explaining that a literal "free lunch" was once offered by corner saloons to drinking patrons — grounding a now-famous economic cliché in a specific, dated cultural memory rather than assuming universal recognition.

- **Closing on the French proverb "plus ça change"**: Graham ends the talk by naming and translating one of his favorite clichés — the French saying "the more it changes the more it's the same thing" — and explicitly argues that its usual emphasis (everything really stays the same) is backwards for his purposes: it is precisely "the more it changes" that lets him predict the market's essential bull/bear character will persist even as the economy transforms.

- **title**: Normal earning power versus optimum earning power — question: What is the sustainable, normalized earning capacity of a business absent extraordinary conditions?, test: Separate cyclical peaks (boom earnings) from the earnings sustainable in peacetime or normal demand; value the normal capacity, then add explicit premiums for temporary favorable conditions, evidence: Proper valuation requires starting with normal earning power, applying a conservative multiplier, then separately accounting for the excess from boom conditions, anchor: business-model
  > "Thus, if you want to attempt a serious evaluation of a company like American Radiator, the only proper method is to take what you would assume to be its normal earning power, not its optimum earning power, evaluate that, and then add to it a fair allowance for the fact that it is facing some very good years." @lines 109–112

- **title**: Persistent overvaluation of "excess working capital" without a return path — question: Should excess working capital be added dollar-for-dollar to a company's valuation, regardless of whether it will actually flow back to security holders?, test: Before crediting excess working capital to valuation, trace its stated use (debt paydown, distribution, retained for operations) and only credit the portion genuinely benefiting the security being valued, evidence: Graham deducted roughly $1 million from the SEC's Childs valuation because part of the "excess" working capital was earmarked to pay off old debt rather than flow to stockholders directly — a valuation adjustment invisible unless you trace where the cash actually goes.

## Metrics found
- **name**: P/E multiplier for normal earning power (Graham's American Radiator example) — formula: Stock value = Normal annual earnings × multiplier; multiplier range 12–15 for established, well-regarded companies; lower multipliers justified by historical volatility, reading: Graham applied 12–15x multiplier to $1.00 of normal post-war earnings for American Radiator, yielding $12–15 base value; then added 3–4x temporary boom earnings premium ($0.75 × 4 = $3) for total $15–18 valuation, anchor: business-model
  > "I think the multiplier would be somewhere between 12 and 15. That is higher than the company's past record would justify, -- but the American Radiator has some advantages in being a large and strong company, well thought of, and which many years ago was a very large earner." @lines 164–167
- **name**: Boom-period earnings premium (Graham's method) — formula: Excess annual earnings during boom × temporary duration multiplier (Graham used 4× for American Radiator building boom earnings); added to normal-earning-power valuation, reading: Graham treated temporary boom earnings conservatively: if normal post-war earnings were $1.00 and boom earnings added $0.75, he applied only 4× to the excess for a $3 premium, refusing to use the standard 12–15× multiplier that would suggest permanence, anchor: business-model
  > "To that you would add an allowance for the boomtime earnings, which are 75 cents a share over expected normal. If you multiply this by four you are again pretty liberal; that will give you three dollars extra." @lines 170–171

- **name**: Practical valuation adjustment for restricted excess working capital — formula: SEC/nominal enterprise value − (portion of "excess" working capital earmarked for debt repayment or other non-distributable use), reading: Graham's own $9 million "practical" valuation for Childs deliberately undercut the SEC's $10 million by roughly $1 million because part of the excess working capital was committed to retiring old debt rather than benefiting the security holder directly, benchNotes: Applies whenever a valuation includes "excess" cash/working capital; verify the actual use of funds before crediting the full amount.
  > "Thus our own "practical" valuation would tend to be $9 million rather than the $10 million found by the SEC." @lines 27–28

## Other

- **Graham revisiting his own prior valuation against real court and market outcomes**: Graham opens the lecture by directly comparing his own earlier $9 million Childs estimate, the SEC's $10 million figure, and the Federal Court's subsequent $9.98 million finding, then checks all three against the actual trading prices of the reorganized securities — a rare, explicit self-audit of a forecast against real-world resolution within the same course.
  > "Since we discussed the matter two weeks ago, the Federal Court has approved the Childs plan, based upon the modified proposals of the trustee; and it has apparently placed the stock equity at $9.98 million, which is $300,000 less than the amount that the SEC found." @lines 29–31

- **A concrete illustration of speculative price range as "fundamentally justified"**: Graham uses American Radiator's actual price history — a low of $3.75 in 1942 versus a plausible $30 in a good year — to make the vivid, almost paradoxical point that both a rock-bottom price and a high speculative price can be simultaneously "fundamentally justified" for the same cyclical security, depending on where in the cycle the market sits.
  > "Just as it can sell at four dollars in bad year, it can easily sell at $30 in a good year, and both prices would be fundamentally justified." @lines 201–202

- **Live classroom Q&A preserved verbatim, showing Graham inviting challenge to his own framework**: The transcript preserves an extended back-and-forth in which a student directly questions whether Graham's five-year earnings-forecast rule conflicts with his boom/normal-earnings framework for the building industry, and Graham concedes the point and explains the exception — a clear instance of him welcoming and validating a student's critical pushback rather than deflecting it.
  > "Yes, you are right in making that point." @lines 185

## Other
- **The "gold dollars for 50 cents" parable**: Graham's central rhetorical device in this piece — recasting a company trading below its net current assets as literally buying gold dollars at a discount — to dramatize why rational buyers should be rushing in, and to explain why they aren't: the "strings attached" of not controlling the corporate cash.
  > "If gold dollars without any strings attached could actually be purchased for 50 cents, plenty of publicity and plenty of buying power would quickly be marshaled to take advantage of the bargain." @lines 18-19
- **The grocery-store test for governance accountability**: Graham reaches for a plain, relatable small-business image — nobody would let a hired manager unilaterally decide whether to shutter a failing grocery store — to argue that public-company shareholders are wrongly ceding an equivalent decision (liquidate vs. continue) entirely to salaried officials.
  > "If you owned a grocery store that was doing badly, you wouldn't leave it to the paid manager to decide whether to keep it going or to shut up shop." @lines 37-39
- **Josh Billings and "sacrificing all his wife's relations"**: Graham invokes the 19th-century American humorist Josh Billings (Henry Wheeler Shaw), known for tongue-in-cheek patriotic aphorisms, to satirize corporate officials who keep loss-making businesses alive at stockholders' expense the way a blustering patriot claims willingness to sacrifice everyone else's relatives but his own.
  > "Like Josh Billings, who in patriotic zeal stood ready to sacrifice all his wife's relations on the altar of his county, officials are willing to sacrifice their stockholders' last dollar to kept he business going." @lines 88-90
- **A rare first-person admission of Graham's own board memberships**: Graham steps outside the analyst's third-person voice to disclose, briefly and directly, that he personally sat on several corporate boards and drew on that lived experience to describe how director loyalty to management operates in practice — an unusually candid biographical aside for a Forbes column.
  > "Directors are not dishonest, but they are human. The writer, being himself a member of several boards, knows something of this subject from personal experience." @lines 101-102
- **Closing on the "which is right" framing device**: The article ends with an editorial sidebar structured as a formal debate ("Which Is Right--the Stock Market or Corporation Management?"), and Forbes explicitly frames this as the third and final installment of Graham's 1932 series reaching "down to the very roots of the present troublous situation" — a marker of how this trilogy was packaged and sold to readers at the time.
  > "Forbes presents herewith the third, and last, article in this series by Mr. Graham, which reaches down to the very roots of the present troublous situation." @lines 154-155

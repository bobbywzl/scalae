## Concepts

- **title**: Margin of Safety — test: enterprise value far exceeds security price; evidence: security offers protection from unforeseen losses through conservative valuation approach
  > "Its chief reliance is on a margin of safety that grows out of a small ratio of debt to total real value of the enterprise." @line 98-99

- **title**: Individual Risk vs. Aggregate Risk — test: diversified portfolio risk differs from single security; evidence: single stocks carry higher risk than single bonds, but diversified stocks historically carried less aggregate risk
  > "This point grows out of the basic difference between individual risk and overall or group risk." @line 154

- **title**: Pathology of Market Prices — test: understand reasons securities trade below value; evidence: causes include depressed earnings, low dividends, excess cash, litigation, capital structure issues
  > "They range from obvious causes, such as an unduly low dividend or a temporary setback in earnings, to more subtle and special conditions such as too much common stock in the capital structure or even too much cash in the bank." @line 217-220

- **title**: Prescientific vs. Scientific Investment Methods — test: methodology rigor and measurability; evidence: bond analysis achieves near-actuarial precision; growth analysis lacks dependable measurement standards
  > "In the growth stock field, the concept of margin of safety loses the clarity and the primacy it enjoys in those other two classes of security analysis." @line 279-281

- **title**: The Fire-Insurance Analogy for Diversified Common Stocks — question: does the market systematically overprice the "insurance premium" it demands for holding common stocks, in the same way individuals overpay for fire insurance relative to actuarial exposure?, test: compare the historical realized return on a diversified common-stock portfolio against the return implied by its true aggregate risk, evidence: Graham explicitly draws the fire-insurance parallel — people pay roughly double their actuarial exposure for fire insurance out of individual-risk aversion, and diversified stocks appear to have paid at least double their true aggregate risk over time
  > "People pay about twice as much for fire insurance as their own actuarially determined exposure would indicate--because they cannot soundly afford to carry the individual risk themselves." @line 169-171

- **title**: The Inverted, Organic Relationship Between Growth and Undervaluation — question: is the market's fixation on a subset of "growth" companies structurally connected to systematic underpricing of the "nongrowth" companies left behind?, test: use the minimum business value of non-favored-group enterprises as a scientific standard to measure the distorting size of the growth premium, evidence: Graham frames growth's pull as "a tidal pull which causes high tides in one area... and low tides in another area," illustrated with three named California companies (Roos Brothers undervalued vs. Superior Oil/Kern County Land overvalued)
  > "There is undoubtedly an organic but inverted relationship between the growth stock concept and the theory of undervalued securities." @line 299-301

- **title**: Diversification's Reversed Status Between Undervalued and Growth Investing — question: does diversification serve the same protective function in growth investing as it does in bond and undervalued-security investing, or does concentrated selectivity actually make more sense there?, test: compare whether the "actuarial element" (many similar independent cases pooled) is present or structurally absent in each category, evidence: Graham argues that in growth investing, selectivity is so prominent it can justify concentrating in "one best or a relatively few best baskets" — the reverse of the diversification imperative in bonds and undervalued issues
  > "in the growth field the element of selectivity is so" @line 286-288

## Metrics

- **name**: High-Grade Corporate Bond Default Rate — formula: approximately 1/2 percent annual default probability (analogous to mortality tables); reading: justifies risk-yield differential between top corporate bonds and government securities
  > "Such a figure, say 1/2%, might then properly measure the risk and yield differential between the strongest corporate bonds and U. S. Government obligations." @line 113-116

- **name**: Dow-Jones Long-Term Growth Rate — formula: one-third appreciation every ten years, equivalent to 2.90% annual compound return plus all dividends; reading: diversified equity investor receives principal growth matching savings bonds rate plus full dividend income
  > "Both the upper and lower lines happen to rise at the rate of one third every ten years." @line 175-176

- **name**: Actuarial Mortality-Rate Analogy for Bond Default Risk — formula: comparable "mortality rate" for corporate enterprises in best financial/operating health, modeled on the ~4-per-1,000 (0.4%/year) mortality rate for men aged 35 used in life insurance pricing, reading: Graham draws a direct structural parallel between insuring a $1,000 life for a $34 annual premium and lending $1,000 on a bond paying $35 per year, benchNotes: this is the conceptual scaffolding underneath the 1/2% default-rate metric — the analogy itself, not just its numeric output, is offered as evidence that bond investment can approach actuarial science
  > "The calculated mortality rate for men aged 35 is about 4 out of 1,000, or 4/10% per year." @line 108-109

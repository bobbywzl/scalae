## Concepts
- **title**: Speculative component in valuation — question: How much of a security's quoted price rests on changeable psychology versus durable economic reality?, test: Decompose price into two layers: investment value and speculative premium, evidence: Quality stocks carry embedded speculation even in blue chips; history shows wide price swings despite stable earnings, anchor: business-model
  > "Of the price of $38, which it averaged in 1939, we said the analyst might conclude that about $25 a share represented the investment component and as much as $13 a share represented the speculative component." @line 82

- **title**: Intelligent versus unintelligent speculation — question: Can an analyst distinguish sound speculation from mere guesswork through experience and calculation?, test: Measure the mathematical odds of success against the market price implied odds; only commit when odds favor the speculation, evidence: Two investments at similar prices can have radically different leverage profiles and protection, anchor: business-model
  > "One turns out to be an intelligent and the other an unintelligent speculation." @line 252

- **title**: Transformation of speculation into investment through diversification — question: Can independent speculative bets become investment-grade through portfolio assembly and the law of averages?, test: Assemble multiple uncorrelated speculations where aggregate probability favors gain, evidence: Ten such ventures with fifty-fifty odds per venture yields fifty percent aggregate return, anchor: business-model
  > "For in ten such operations you would get $50 back for an investment of $30, if you have average luck." @line 312

- **title**: Timing via valuation, not market prediction — question: Can the analyst avoid the impossible task of predicting markets by instead buying cheap and selling dear?, test: Apply security analysis to establish buy and sell zones; execute when price enters those zones, evidence: Requires no opinion on market direction; safety comes from price relative to value, anchor: business-model
  > "That sounds like timing; but when you consider it you will see that it is not really timing at all but rather the purchase and sale of securities by the method of valuation." @line 441

- **title**: Market forecasting as a self-neutralizing zero-sum activity among experts — question: Does the sheer skill and volume of expert market forecasters cause their collective efforts to cancel out, making consistent market forecasting an inherently unrewarding pursuit regardless of individual skill?, test: Aggregate the directional calls of many skilled forecasters over time and check whether the aggregate signal adds information beyond what is already in the price, evidence: Graham argues the market "already reflects, almost at every time, everything that the experts can reliably say about its future," so genuinely new-information forecasts are right only about half the time by construction.
  > "The trouble with market forecasting is not that it is done by unintelligent and unskillful people. Quite to the contrary, the trouble is that it is done by so many really expert people that their efforts constantly neutralize each other, and end up almost exactly in zero." @line 422

- **title**: Process-quality standard for judging speculation, independent of realized outcome — question: Should a speculative decision be judged by whether it made money, or by whether it was grounded in sufficient study and sound judgment at the time it was made?, test: Separate ex-post outcome from ex-ante process quality when reviewing a speculative decision; judge "wisdom" solely by the second, evidence: Graham's bridge analogy — "playing a hand right rather than... playing it successfully" — makes explicit that a well-reasoned speculation that loses money was not therefore unwise, and a poorly-reasoned one that wins was not therefore wise.
  > "A speculation is unwise only if it is made on insufficient study and by poor judgment." @line 468

## Metrics
- **name**: Speculative component (in dollars and percent) — formula: Market price minus calculated investment value, reading: A large speculative component signals vulnerability to sentiment reversals; one-third of price for highest-grade stocks indicates pervasive speculation, anchor: business-model
  > "Of the price of $38, which it averaged in 1939, we said the analyst might conclude that about $25 a share represented the investment component and as much as $13 a share represented the speculative component." @line 82

- **name**: Leverage coverage — formula: Asset appreciation required to reach parity with market equity price, reading: Allegheny required seventy percent asset increase to reach market price; low coverage indicates unintelligent speculation; high coverage indicates intelligent leverage, anchor: business-model
  > "The fact of the matter is you would need a 70 per cent increase in the value of the Allegheny portfolio merely to be even with the market price of the common as far as asset value coverage is concerned." @line 245

- **name**: Mathematical odds test — formula: Probability of success measured from experience versus odds implied by market price, reading: Odds must favor the speculation before capital commits; diversification of favorable-odds independent bets yields long-term gain, anchor: business-model
  > "If the probabilities, as measure by our mathematical test, are definitely in favor of the speculation, then we can transform these separate intelligent speculations into investment by the simple device of diversification." @line 303

- **name**: Diversified favorable-odds payoff arithmetic — formula: N independent bets at even odds, each costing $C with payoff $P on success ≈ (N/2 × $P) recovered against (N × $C) invested, reading: Graham's Electric Bond and Share Stubs example: ten $3 positions (aggregate $30 invested) with a 50-50 shot at a $10 payoff each returns $50 back "if you have average luck" — a ~67% aggregate gain built entirely from individually uncertain, even-odds bets, benchNotes: Requires true independence between the bets (see the "real diversification" directive); the arithmetic breaks down if the underlying success factor is shared across positions.
  > "For in ten such operations you would get $50 back for an investment of $30, if you have average luck." @line 312

## Other

- **The "Oil Discovered in Hell" joke as a parable of herd behavior**: Graham tells the full afterlife joke about an oil man tricking his way into a full Heaven by shouting "Oil discovered in hell!" — and then following the stampede himself on the theory that "there may be some truth in that rumor after all" — using it as a vivid, memorable parable for why even self-aware, experienced security analysts keep following the speculative crowd against their own better judgment.
  > "It reminds me of the story you all know of the oil man who went to Heaven and asked St. Peter to let him in." @line 398-399

- **The bridge-player's-wife story as a parable of process over outcome**: Graham tells a short marital anecdote — a weak bridge player triumphantly makes a grand slam his wife had visibly doubted, only for her to reply "If you had played it right you would have lost it" — as a compact illustration that a good outcome does not retroactively validate bad process, directly reinforcing his point that speculative wisdom is judged by analysis quality, not results.
  > "If you had played it right you would have lost it." @line 477-478

- **Reference to Balzac's The Search for the Absolute as a literary parallel to market forecasting**: Graham draws an explicit literary analogy between market forecasters and the obsessive doctor in Balzac's novel who spends his life pursuing something wonderful he will never find, framing the pursuit of consistently accurate market timing as a comparable, ultimately fruitless quest — an unusual moment of Graham reaching outside finance into classic literature to make his point.
  > "Reading recently the biography of Balzac, I recalled that novel of his called, The Search for the Absolute, which some of you may have read. In it a very intelligent doctor spends all his time looking for something which would be wonderful if he found it, but which he never finds." @line 429-431

- **Graham's closing personal reflection spanning his full 1914-1947 Wall Street career**: In his final lecture of the series, Graham steps back to compare Wall Street's ethics, information quality, and analytical technique across his over-thirty-year career, concluding that nearly everything has improved except "human nature" itself and the clarity of the investment/speculation distinction — a rare, explicitly autobiographical closing statement summing up decades of firsthand observation rather than a single case study.
  > "If you can throw your mind, as I can, as far back as 1914, you would be struck by some extraordinary differences in Wall Street then and today." @line 501-502

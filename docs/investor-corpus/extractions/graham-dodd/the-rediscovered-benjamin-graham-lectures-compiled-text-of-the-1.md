## Question patterns
- **pattern**: When a company or analyst cites an "earning power" figure, is it a trailing result, a next-twelve-months forecast, or a normalized multi-year average — and does the multiplier applied to it match which one it is? — askWhen: any valuation claim uses "earning power," "normalized earnings," or an implied multiple without stating its time horizon, anchor: business-model
  > "My suggestion is that it would be a five-year period, and that when we speak of future earning power of a company, we should have in mind ordinarily the average earnings over the next five years." @line 792

- **pattern**: Are the margins or earnings being capitalized here a temporary seller's-market or boom-cycle peak, or a defensible full-cycle normal — and has the analysis re-based to the cycle-normal figure before applying a multiplier? — askWhen: a business is riding a capacity shortage, a housing/commodity boom, a war, or any other period of abnormally favorable pricing power, anchor: business-model
  > "The earnings for the building boom should be evaluated pretty much in the same way as we were accustomed to evaluating war earnings, that is to say, by assuming that they were to last for a limited number of years." @line 1264

- **pattern**: Is management's stated reason for a low dividend or heavy earnings retention (conservatism, special hazards, superior expertise) specific to this company's economics, or a generic argument that would equally justify paying nothing at all? — askWhen: a demonstrably profitable company retains earnings well beyond any stated reinvestment plan, anchor: culture
  > "a stockholder can suffer from failure to pay out earnings, when they are realized, nearly as much as he suffers from the failure to realize earnings." @line 1964

- **pattern**: Does the source presenting this valuation or policy argument disclose its own financial stake and any dispute with the subject company's management, and does the argument still hold once that interest is priced in? — askWhen: weighing an analyst note, activist letter, or advocacy piece that argues for a specific valuation or corporate-policy outcome, anchor: culture
  > "my investment company has an interest in the New Amsterdam Casualty Company, and I have had a dispute with the management as to proper dividend policy." @line 2045

- **pattern**: Before sizing a speculative position, what explicit probability estimate underlies it, and does the probability-weighted payoff across a diversified set of similar bets favor the buyer? — askWhen: evaluating a binary or event-driven situation (litigation outcome, stub security, regulatory ruling) priced as a speculation rather than a standard valuation case, anchor: business-model
  > "If we were to assume that the Electric Bond and Share Stubs have a 50-50 chance of getting the ten dollar premium, then he would conclude that at three dollars a share they are an intelligent speculation." @line 2297

- **pattern**: Is a stock disfavored (or favored) purely because its near-term prospects seem obviously bad (or obviously good) — and has that obvious-prospects judgment actually been tested against a demonstrated, quantitative value differential rather than accepted at face value? — askWhen: a stock is being screened out or in based on a widely-shared narrative about industry/company prospects, anchor: business-model
  > "National Distillers went up from the low of 1940 more than fivefold recently, and is now selling nearly four times its 1940 price. The buyer of United Aircraft would have had a very small profit at its best price and would now have a loss of one third of his money." @line 126-128

- **pattern**: When a growth stock is priced at a multiplier many times that of an otherwise-similar-earnings peer, what specific, testable evidence justifies projecting that company's superior growth many years into the future rather than the standard five-to-seven-year window? — askWhen: comparing two companies with similar current per-share earnings but wildly divergent P/E multiples, anchor: business-model

- **pattern**: Does the analyst's growth-stock recommendation specify, in dollar terms, exactly how much of the current price is being paid for the growth factor versus what the stock would be worth at an average, non-growth multiplier? — askWhen: reviewing any "buy for growth" recommendation before accepting or rejecting it, anchor: business-model

## Search directives
- **directive**: For a holding company, railroad, or any parent with a large minority stake in an unconsolidated affiliate, pull the affiliate's own undistributed earnings and the parent's other off-income-statement asset income (land departments, joint-facility accounts), and add the parent's pro-rata share back onto its reported per-share earnings before valuing it. — queryShapes: ["<PARENT>'s ownership percentage in <AFFILIATE> and <AFFILIATE>'s undistributed earnings per share, by year", "does <PARENT> have off-income-statement asset departments (land, real estate, joint facilities) with separate surplus credits?"], sourcePriority: parent and affiliate annual reports; equity-method/undistributed-earnings footnotes; regulatory filings for railroads and utilities

- **directive**: When a company retains most of its earnings, compare its capital-to-business ratio (invested assets or equity per dollar of premiums/sales) against its own ratio from decades earlier, to test whether retained capital is funding growth or simply accumulating idle. — queryShapes: ["<COMPANY> invested assets ÷ stockholders' capital, current period vs. ~15-20 years earlier", "<COMPANY> premiums or sales written per dollar of stockholders' equity, trend over two decades"], sourcePriority: historical annual reports spanning 15-20+ years; same-industry peer filings with materially different payout ratios
  > "In 1927 they had $1.45 of invested assets per dollar of stockholder's capital, and now they have only $1.18." @line 1885

- **directive**: Before pricing a company off its most recent quarter or half-year margin, pull its net-margin-after-tax history over the prior five and ten years and check whether the current period's margin is explained by a general shortage or seller's-market condition rather than a durable competitive change. — queryShapes: ["<COMPANY> net margin after tax by year, trailing 10 years, vs. most recent half-year", "supply/demand or capacity-shortage conditions cited in <INDUSTRY> commentary for <PERIOD>"], sourcePriority: multi-year income-statement history; industry capacity and shortage commentary contemporaneous with the period under review
  > "In the six months ended June 1946 the company earned 15 per cent on its sales after taxes. It had previously tended to earn somewhere around three or four per cent on sales after taxes." @line 209

- **directive**: When comparing two companies in the same industry with similar working-capital or asset bases but a large disparity in market price, check both companies' balance-sheet trend (asset markdowns vs. markups) over the same recent period rather than the current market price alone — companies that wrote assets down aggressively during a downturn can look artificially cheap or expensive relative to peers who marked assets up. — queryShapes: ["<COMPANY A> and <COMPANY B> fixed-asset markdown/markup history over the same multi-year window", "does one peer show an arbitrary plant/asset mark-up shortly before a stock offering?"], sourcePriority: comparative balance sheet history for named industry peers
  > "During the period in which Taylorcraft was marking up its fixed assets by means of this appraisal figure, the large companies like United Aircraft and Curtiss-Wright marked down their plants to practically nothing" @line 263-265

- **directive**: To find a company's "true earnings" independent of accounting or tax-driven distortions in reported net income, use the comparative balance-sheet method: take the equity at period-end minus equity at period-start, adjust for capital transactions (new stock sales, etc.), and add back dividends paid — the resulting figure often diverges materially from the single-year reported earnings figure. — queryShapes: ["<COMPANY> equity at start vs end of <PERIOD>, plus dividends paid, adjusted for capital transactions", "does <COMPANY>'s reported EPS diverge materially from its balance-sheet-derived earnings over the same period?"], sourcePriority: comparative balance sheets across the full period under review, not single-year income statements alone
  > "you take the equity for the stock at the end of the period, you subtract the equity at the beginning of the period, and the difference is the gain. That gain should be adjusted for items that do not relate to earnings, and there should be added back the dividends paid." @line 332-335

- **directive**: When a company's near-term (next-12-months) prospects are used as the sole basis for a buy or sell recommendation, check what the current market price already implies about multi-year forward earnings before concluding the near-term view is not already priced in. — queryShapes: ["<COMPANY> current P/E vs peer average — how many years of the stated near-term improvement does the premium already imply?", "has <COMPANY>'s price already moved to reflect the anticipated near-term change?"], sourcePriority: current multiple relative to peer/historical multiple, cross-referenced against the stated near-term catalyst
  > "the price of 34 for U.S. Plywood might have discounted very good earnings for three years, and the price of National Department Stores might theoretically have discounted poor earnings for three years." @line 1617-1619

## Concepts
- **title**: Continuity and mean reversion of market price levels — question: Does a market index (or a stock) that has broken to a new price level tend to hold it permanently once a disruption (war, boom, "new era" thinking) has passed, or does it revert toward its long-run historical range?, test: chart an index's price and per-unit earnings across 20-30+ years spanning multiple regime shocks; check whether new highs or lows are ever permanently held rather than round-tripped, evidence: Dow-Jones earnings centered near $10/unit from 1915 to 1945 despite year-to-year swings to $22 and to zero, and the index price never permanently escaping its historical fluctuation band across two world wars and a depression
  > "The extraordinary thing about the securities market, if you judge it over a long period of years, is the fact that it does not go off on tangents permanently, but it remains in continuous orbit." @line 59

- **title**: Deceptive selectivity — obvious prospects mispredict returns — question: Does buying securities with self-evidently good near-term prospects (and avoiding those with self-evidently bad ones) actually produce superior returns versus buying on demonstrated quantitative value differentials?, test: compare subsequent price performance of a stock disfavored for weak-seeming prospects against one favored for strong-seeming prospects, across a full cycle, evidence: National Distillers, sold down on the assumption wartime conditions would hurt a "luxury" whiskey business, later rose several-fold, while United Aircraft, favored for wartime demand, delivered a small gain or a loss over the same span
  > "No doubt there was a general feeling that the company's prospects were not good, primarily because it was thought that war would not be a very good thing for a luxury type of business such as whiskey is politely considered to be." @line 119

- **title**: Dividend policy as an independent source of shareholder harm — question: Can a profitable, well-run company still damage shareholders purely through a dividend policy that retains earnings far beyond payout precedent, independent of the earnings themselves?, test: compare two near-identical businesses (same line of business, similar per-share earnings and assets) that differ chiefly in payout ratio; check whether the low-payout name trades at a persistent price discount to the high-payout name, evidence: New Amsterdam Casualty ($1 dividend on a $4.33 five-year average) traded near 26-28 against U.S. Fidelity & Guaranty ($2 dividend, near-identical business) near 45
  > "a stockholder can suffer from failure to pay out earnings, when they are realized, nearly as much as he suffers from the failure to realize earnings." @line 1964

- **title**: Conventional (quality/prospects) analysis versus penetrating (value) analysis — question: should an analyst's primary discipline be identifying "good companies" with good prospects, or identifying gaps between price and appraised value regardless of narrative quality?, test: for any "buy" call, check whether the analyst states an appraised value range and the amount being paid for quality or growth, or merely asserts favorable prospects with no value anchor, evidence: Graham ranks plain "good stocks at fair prices" as the most reliably useful of the conventional approaches precisely because it still carries a price discipline, unlike unconditional growth-stock or near-term-prospect calls
  > "I suggest that there are two fundamentally different approaches that the analyst may take to securities as a whole. The first I call the conventional one, and that is based primarily on quality and on prospects. The second I call, in complimentary fashion, the penetrating one, and that is based upon value." @line 1521

- **title**: The Speculative Component of a Quoted Price, Decomposed — question: even within the single highest-grade investment-quality common stock, can a meaningful fraction of the quoted price be attributed to a psychologically-driven "speculative component" separate from its dividend-based investment value?, test: separately capitalize the dividend/earnings-based investment value and compare it to the actual quoted price; the residual is the speculative component, evidence: General Electric's 1939 average price of $38 splits into ~$25 investment value and ~$13 speculative component — roughly one-third of the price of the era's exemplary blue chip
  > "Of the price of $38, which it averaged in 1939, we said the analyst might conclude that about $25 a share represented the investment component and as much as $13 a share represented the speculative component." @line 2130-2132

- **title**: Intelligent vs. Unintelligent Speculation as a Mathematical, Not Moral, Distinction — question: does "speculation" as a category deserve blanket disapproval, or can it be split into intelligent speculation (favorable, calculable odds, diversified) and unintelligent speculation (leverage paid for at a price that erases any realistic edge)?, test: compare the price paid for a leveraged claim on assets against the size of the leverage and the odds of the claim paying off; check whether a diversified set of similar bets would show positive expected value, evidence: Graham contrasts General Shareholdings common (leveraged claim bought below its mathematical value — intelligent) against Allegheny Corp common (similar leveraged structure but priced so far "under water" that a 70% asset-value gain would be needed just to break even — unintelligent)
  > "Thus, from the analytical standpoint, while Allegheny and General Shareholdings represent approximately the same general picture, there is a very wide quantitative disparity between the two. One turns out to be an intelligent and the other an unintelligent speculation." @line 2275-2277

- **title**: Diversification Only Works When the Underlying Risk Factors Actually Differ — question: does buying multiple similar speculative positions constitute real diversification, or is it merely a larger single bet if the same underlying factor drives all of them?, test: check whether the sources of potential success or failure differ meaningfully across the positions in a "diversified" book, evidence: Graham explicitly rejects buying ten near-identical Electric Bond and Share stub situations as true diversification, calling it "practically the same thing as buying ten shares" of one security — real diversification requires genuinely independent risk factors
  > "you are not really diversifying if you went into ten Electric Bond and Share situations -- all substantially the same" @line 2364-2366

- **title**: Wide Price Fluctuation in "Investment-Grade" Common Stocks as a Post-WWI Structural Shift — question: has the historical assumption that high-quality dividend-paying common stocks fluctuate only modestly in price (allowing investors to largely ignore price and focus on the dividend) remained true since World War I?, test: compare the price range of a stable-earnings, stable-dividend utility (Consolidated Edison) across two crisis periods — one pre-WWI-era-style (1919-23) and one later (1936-38) — with nearly identical earnings behavior in both, evidence: in 1919-23, despite earnings collapsing to $1.40/share, the price range was a modest 71-106 on a maintained $7 dividend; in 1936-38, with no material earnings change and even a dividend increase, the price still swung from ~50 down to 17 — a qualitatively wider fluctuation despite steadier fundamentals
  > "The much wider fluctuations in investment common stocks that have come about since World War I have made it practically impossible for buyers of common stocks to disregard price changes." @line 2166-2168

## Metrics
- **name**: Hidden/unconsolidated equity add-back — formula: reported EPS + (ownership % × affiliate's undistributed earnings per share) + other off-income-statement asset-department income, reading: when the add-back materially changes the earnings picture, treat the standalone reported figure as understating true earning power and re-derive value from the combined figure, benchNotes: Northern Pacific's reported ~$6.20/share (1941-45 average) becomes ~$11.46/share once its 48.5% Burlington stake's undistributed profits, Spokane-Portland-Seattle equity, and land-department income are added back — nearly double the headline number
  > "Thus you find that there is what used to be called a “hidden equity” of about $26 a share additional in those five years, making a total of about $53 that has gone back into the stockholders' account for Northern Pacific as compared with the pre-war period." @line 538

- **name**: Net-deductions fixed-charge coverage (rental/joint-facility-adjusted) — formula: net income after taxes ÷ "net deductions" (bond interest + preferred dividends + net equipment-rental/joint-facility payments, replacing the conventional fixed-charge figure), reading: recompute coverage on this net-deductions basis before trusting a headline fixed-charge-coverage ratio, since a company receiving net rental credits can look worse under conventional accounting than one paying large net rentals while actually being better covered, benchNotes: on a net-deductions basis Northern Pacific's relative coverage improved versus Southern Pacific's compared with what the conventional fixed-charge figures implied
  > "Those of you who have studied our text on Security Analysis will recall our reference to the “net deductions method” in which you replace fixed charges by a figure representing the difference between the net after taxes and the balance for stock." @line 626

- **name**: Invested-assets-per-dollar-of-capital (capital-intensity drift) — formula: total invested assets ÷ stockholders' equity, tracked across decades, reading: a falling ratio over a long period signals capital accumulating faster than the business deployed against it, depressing return on capital even when underwriting or operating margins are unchanged, benchNotes: North River's ratio fell from $1.45 to $1.18 over the roughly eighteen-year comparison Graham cites, alongside premiums-per-dollar-of-capital being cut in half
  > "In 1927 they had $1.45 of invested assets per dollar of stockholder's capital, and now they have only $1.18." @line 1885

- **name**: Speculative component of quoted price — formula: quoted market price − appraised "investment value" (capitalized normalized-earnings or dividend basis) = speculative component, in dollars and as a percent of price, reading: even the highest investment-grade common stock can carry a large speculative component; track the size and trend of this residual as a proxy for how much of a holding's return depends on sentiment rather than analyzable value, benchNotes: General Electric's 1939 average price of $38 splits into roughly $25 investment value plus $13 speculative component (about one-third of price); the stock's subsequent round-trip through 1946 tracked the speculative residual rather than the dividend-based investment value
  > "Of the price of $38, which it averaged in 1939, we said the analyst might conclude that about $25 a share represented the investment component and as much as $13 a share represented the speculative component." @line 2130

- **name**: Comparative Balance-Sheet True-Earnings Reconciliation — formula: True earnings = (period-end equity − period-start equity, adjusted for capital transactions) + dividends paid; compare this to cumulative reported income-statement earnings for the same span to find the "unraveled" divergence, reading: applied to Curtiss-Wright over ten years, the balance-sheet method showed $18.53/share true earnings against $12.28/share reported — a $6.25/share (50%) understatement, driven by war-related reserves later released as current assets; the same method applied to a smaller pair (Transue Williams vs. Buda) in the same lecture found the divergence could run in either direction depending on whether a company's reserves proved necessary or excessive, benchNotes: this is Graham's primary tool for detecting earnings distorted by discretionary reserve accounting, LIFO adoption, or wartime amortization elections — always cross-check the income-statement trend against this balance-sheet-derived figure before trusting reported EPS
  > "In the case of Curtiss-Wright we have as much as $44-million difference between the earnings as shown by the single reports and the earnings as shown by a comparison of surplus and reserves at the beginning and end of the period." @line 307-309

- **name**: Diversified Expected-Value Test for Intelligent Speculation — formula: for a position with a stated probability p of a favorable payoff X (and 1-p of zero/loss), intelligent speculation requires p×X > cost across a genuinely diversified set of similarly-structured but independently-driven bets, reading: applied to Electric Bond & Share Stubs bought at $3 with an assumed 50% chance of a $10 payoff — ten such genuinely diversified bets would return $50 against $30 invested, a favorable expected value, benchNotes: explicitly NOT meant to require Euclidean precision in the probability estimate ("you don't have to be any more accurate for practical action") — the test is whether the odds are conservatively estimated to favor the buyer, not whether the exact probability is known
  > "For in ten such operations you would get $50 back for an investment of $30, if you have average luck." @line 2328-2330

## Other

- **The lecture-opening marriage metaphor recurs as the course's organizing image for the analyst/market relationship** — rather than treating it as a throwaway joke, Graham returns to variants of "ignore the market entirely" vs. "follow it slavishly" as a false binary across multiple lectures, first establishing it in Lecture One as the frame for everything that follows about continuity and deceptive selectivity.
  > "He shouldn't pay too much attention to what the lady says, but he can't afford to ignore it entirely." @line 40

- **A named, dollar-and-cents worked comparison of two nearly-identical insurance companies makes the dividend-policy argument fully auditable** — rather than asserting abstractly that dividend policy affects price, Graham walks through New Amsterdam Casualty (paying $1 on $4.33 average earnings, trading 26-28) against U.S. Fidelity & Guaranty (paying $2 on comparable earnings, trading near 45) as a matched pair differing chiefly in payout ratio.
  > "it turns out that we have an extraordinarily pat comparative example here in the form of another casualty company, called the U.S. Fidelity and Guaranty." @line 1987-1988

- **Graham catalogs, verbatim, the three stock excuses management gives for withholding dividends — and then rebuts each one in turn** — conservatism, special business hazards, and stockholders' inferior understanding of the business — presented as a reusable checklist for evaluating any company's dividend-retention rationale rather than a one-off critique of New Amsterdam specifically.
  > "You will find, if you talk to the management on the subject, that they will give you three reasons for their dividend policy; and if you have done similar missionary work over a period of time, the arguments will sound strangely familiar to you." @line 1997-1999

- **A self-deprecating anecdote (the oil man and St. Peter) illustrates herding behavior in market forecasting** — Graham uses a full comic parable, not just an assertion, to make the point that even security analysts who intellectually know better than to follow speculative crowds nonetheless do so — "there may be some truth in the rumor after all."

- **A second closing anecdote — the bridge-playing husband and wife — reinforces "process over outcome" as the course's final lesson on speculation** — the wife's dry rebuke ("If you had played it right you would have lost it") crystallizes Graham's point that a speculation's wisdom is judged by the soundness of the reasoning at the time, not by whether it happened to pay off.
  > "his wife replied very dourly, "If you had played it right you would have lost it."" @line 2461-2462

- **The final lecture closes with an explicit, dated personal vantage point spanning Graham's entire Wall Street career** — rather than a generic summary, he anchors his closing remarks on ethics and human nature in his own memory of 1914, giving the whole ten-lecture series an autobiographical bookend.
  > "If you can throw your mind, as I can, as far back as 1914, you would be struck by some extraordinary differences in Wall Street then and today." @line 2490-2491

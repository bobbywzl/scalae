## Concepts

- **title**: The investor-speculator distinction in common stock pricing — question: Can you decompose a stock's market price into rational investment value (business earnings discounted) plus speculative value (expected future upside)? Evidence: In Lens form, the "signal" is earning power and asset value; the "noise" is sentiment-driven price swings.
  > "To my mind the most valuable contribution that security analysts could make to the art of investing would be the determination of the investment and speculative components in the current price of any given common stock, so that the intending buyer might have some notion of the risks he is taking as well as what profit he might make." @line 306

- **title**: Cyclical market psychology and valuation extremes — question: What indicators show when excessive optimism (or pessimism) is driving prices beyond justified levels? Evidence: New-issue frenzies, margin borrowing, individual stocks rising 2-6x then falling 50%+, yield inversions between stocks and bonds.
  > "These very wide swings underline the fact that the stock market is basically the same now as it always was, in the sense that it is still subject to very substantial overvaluation at some times and undoubtedly substantial undervaluation at others." @line 198

- **title**: Formula-based portfolio rebalancing to enforce buy-low/sell-high discipline — question: How can mechanical rules (fixed allocation percentages, trigger-based rebalancing) keep investors from emotional decision-making during extremes? Evidence: Yale method (35% stocks, rebalance at +5% or −5%), dollar-cost averaging, fixed 50-50 bond-stock split with threshold rebalancing.
  > "Use a fixed 50-50 division between bonds and stocks. When the market level of the stocks rises to a point where they constitute 55% of the total or maybe 60%, you would then sell out enough to bring your proportion back to 50%, putting the proceeds back into bonds or into savings banks. And conversely when the market went down so that your common-stock proportion had fallen to 45% or 40%, you would use some of your bond money to buy common stocks and bring it back to the 50%." @line 268

- **title**: Skepticism toward forecasting as an investment discipline — question: If a dependable economic or earnings forecast exists, is it already reflected in the price, leaving no edge to exploit? Evidence: 1964 forecast reasoning, the Dow Theory's post-popularity failure, the funds' inability to beat the averages.
  > "to the extent that an economic forecast appears dependable — and it is generally so only for the short term — its effect is likely to be already reflected in the market level, and there is no way to make money from it." @line 234

- **title**: "Bargain issues" priced below working-capital value — question: Does the market price a company at less than the liquidation value of its current assets alone, ignoring fixed assets and goodwill entirely? Evidence: shares selling under net working capital were numerous through 1957 and consistently profitable when diversified.
  > "Buy definitely “bargain issues.” Typically these would be shares that sold for less than their value in working capital alone, with nothing paid for fixed assets and goodwill." @line 316

- **title**: Perpetual insecurity as the baseline condition for investment policy — question: Given atomic-war risk, inflation risk, and market-fluctuation risk are all permanent and unresolvable, how should portfolio policy be structured to survive rather than to predict? Evidence: the 25%-75% bond/stock band framework built explicitly around irreducible uncertainty.
  > "The investor must recognize that there are uncertain and hence speculative elements in any policy he follows — even an all-Government-bond program. He must deal with these uncertainties by a policy of continuous compromise between bonds and common stocks, and by adequate diversification." @line 322

## Metrics

- **name**: Price-to-earnings (P/E) multiple as valuation metric — formula: stock market average price divided by recent average earnings per unit; reading: Graham shows 1949–50 levels at 7x earnings vs 1963 at 20x earnings; bench notes: Graham notes old standards would suggest caution above 12x multiple (8.6% capitalization rate), yet 1963 market at 750 reflects ~20x; he views swings between 7x and 20x as evidence of cyclical mispricing rather than fundamental change.
  > "For example, at the present time, the average earnings for the last ten years are about $33 on the Dow Jones unit and the present rate on high-grade bonds is 4.3%. If you capitalize $33 at 8.6%, which is a multiplier of about 12, you would get a Central Value on the old basis of about 380, as compared with the present price of about 750." @line 148

- **name**: Bond yield versus stock yield spread as valuation signal — formula: interest rate on high-grade bonds minus dividend yield on stock averages; reading: Graham reports bonds yielding 4.3%, stocks yielding ~3.1%, an inversion that used to signal top risk; bench notes: Graham emphasizes this 50-year market rule (stocks should yield more than bonds) broke in 1958 and remained inverted, challenging whether it reflects permanent regime change or over-valuation.
  > "The present relationship gives considerably lower yields on stocks than on bonds. High-grade bonds yield about 4.3% while the stock averages yield a little bit more than 3%." @line 184

- **name**: Working capital value per share as "bargain issue" floor — formula: current assets minus liabilities divided by shares outstanding, compared to market price; reading: Graham identifies "bargain issues" as shares trading below working capital alone; bench notes: Common in 1950s; by 1963 mostly gone; precursor to asset-based value investing; most reliable signal for below-intrinsic pricing.
  > "Typically these would be shares that sold for less than their value in working capital alone, with nothing paid for fixed assets and goodwill. These were quite numerous up to as late as 1957, and were consistently profitable when diversification was observed." @line 316

- **name**: "Graham" Central Value formula (Dow Jones basis) — formula: ten-year average earnings of the 30 Dow Jones stocks, capitalized at twice the high-grade bond interest rate, then optionally marked up 50% for the post-Employment Act depression-insurance adjustment; reading: yields ~380 on the old basis, ~570 with the 50% markup, versus the 1963 market price of 750; bench notes: worked well 1871–1954 but Graham flags that any method's apparent confidence tends to arrive just as new conditions make it stop working.
  > "It was derived from the Central Value average earnings of the 30 stocks in the Dow Jones Industrial Average for ten years past, capitalized at twice the interest rate on high-grade bonds." @line 148

- **name**: Investment-component appraisal for glamour stocks — formula: a conservative capitalization of demonstrated (non-speculative) earning power, stated per share and in aggregate enterprise value, set against the full market price to isolate the speculative remainder; reading: IBM's investment component appraised at not more than $200/share (~$6B enterprise) against a $607 1961 high; International Harvester's investment component pegged at ≥80% of its $55 1961 high; bench notes: the ratio of investment component to total price is itself a risk metric — the smaller the ratio, the larger the speculative bet embedded in the quotation.
  > "I have pointed out that my own conservative appraisal put the investment component in IBM's 1961 price at not more than $200 per share — about $6 billion for the entire enterprise — the remainder of the quotation representing a speculative valuation of the company's undoubtedly brilliant future." @line 308

## Other

- **The Analysts Journal renamed under psychoanalyst confusion**: Graham recounts founding the New York Society of Security Analysts' journal in 1946 under a name so ambiguous it drew mail from the wrong profession, forcing a rename — a small institutional-history anecdote about the birth of the Financial Analysts Journal.
  > "when the New York Society of Security Analysts established a journal in 1946 we gave it the simple title The Analysts Journal . But then we got so many inquiries and even subscriptions from psychoanalysts that we were forced in self-defense to change the name to the present title The Financial Analysts Journal." @line 64

- **The broker's necktie chart and the abandonment of the yield rule**: Graham tells of a broker friend so devoted to the stock-yield-vs-bond-yield rule that he had the relationship printed on neckties imported from Paris and gave them to friends including Graham — then, once the rule stopped working, publicly renounced it in a huge newspaper ad, a vivid symbol of how conviction in a market maxim evaporates exactly when it is most needed.
  > "he actually had the relationship between stock yields and bond yields printed in a nice chart-design on neckties imported from Paris, which he distributed to his friends, including me. For a while I wore this tie at some of my lectures and said that this was the first time that anybody had analyzed the technical position of the stock market from his necktie." @line 178

- **Graham's first (and last) market prediction, age 20, one month on the job**: Graham dates his personal history precisely: his first employer, Newburger, Henderson & Loeb, put him in charge of the firm's daily market letter after just one month of Wall Street experience — an assignment he found so uncomfortable he never made another market prediction for the rest of his career.
  > "the last time I made any stock-market predictions was in the year 1914, when my firm Newburger, Henderson & Loeb" @line 208

- **Nearly fifty years in Wall Street, and less certainty about forecasting, not more**: A late-career aphorism about the relationship between experience and epistemic humility — the more decades Graham spent in the market, the less confident he became about predicting it and the more confident he became about prescribing investor conduct.
  > "In my nearly fifty years of experience in Wall Street I've found that I know less and less about what the stock market is going to do but I know more and more about what investors ought to do; and that's a pretty vital change in attitude." @line 258

- **"Do you want to eat well or to sleep well?"**: Graham closes the lecture with an old Wall Street story about a broker who, when asked to recommend stocks, always turned the question back on the client's temperament rather than the market's prospects — Graham's chosen parable for reconciling return-seeking with risk tolerance.
  > "he always asked in return, “What is your preference? Do you want to eat well or to sleep well?” I am optimist enough to believe that by following sound policies almost any investor — even in this insecure world — should be able to eat well enough without having to lose any sleep." @line 324

- **South Sea Bubble as three-century anchor for market psychology**: Graham reaches back to 1720 to argue that alternations between excessive optimism and excessive pessimism are not a modern artifact but a nearly 300-year constant of markets, using Cowles and Standard & Poor's price records back to 1871 as the modern data spine.
  > "the nature of the market has not changed from its earliest times, as shown in our records that go back at least to the South Sea Bubble in 1720, practically 300 years." @line 124

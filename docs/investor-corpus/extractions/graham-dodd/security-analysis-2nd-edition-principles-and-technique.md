## Concepts
- **title**: Intrinsic value — question: what value is justified by the facts (assets, earnings, dividends, definite prospects) as distinct from the market's current quotation? test: does a "range of approximate value" clearly bracket the price above or below it? evidence: Wright Aeronautical (1922 vs. 1929) and J. I. Case (1933) show the range narrowing or widening with the certainty of the facts.
  > "it is a great mistake to imagine that intrinsic value is as definite and as determinable as is the market price." @lines 1298-1300
- **title**: Margin of safety — question: how large is the cushion of earning power or asset value above the fixed claims a purchase is subject to? test: coverage of fixed charges (and, for preferred stock, charges plus preferred dividends) against minimum benchmarks that scale with instability of the business. evidence: pyramided 1929 utility bonds that relied on "certain" future growth instead of a present margin collapsed when growth stopped.
  > "As the degree of instability increases, it must be offset by a greater margin of safety to make sure that interest charges will be met" @lines 4955-4957
- **title**: Earning power vs. simple average — question: does the year-by-year record show a "normal" or "modal" tendency, or is the multi-year average just an arithmetical resultant of disconnected figures? test: compare the spread of individual years around the average (S. H. Kress's tight cluster vs. Hudson Motors' wide scatter around similar averages). evidence: the qualitative stability of the underlying business, not the arithmetic, determines which case applies.
  > "The concept of earning power has a definite and important place in investment theory." @lines 20707-20708
- **title**: Optimum capitalization structure — question: does the company's mix of bonds, preferred, and common represent a suitable amount of leverage, or a speculative/top-heavy structure that throws every security in the capital stack into doubt? test: could the senior securities safely be issued and bought as investments on their own quantitative merits? evidence: hypothetical Companies A/B/C — value is destroyed, not created, once the senior layer exceeds what can safely be sold as an investment.
  > "The optimum capitalization structure for any enterprise includes senior securities to the extent that they may safely be issued and bought for investment." @lines 22752-22754
- **title**: Preferred-stock anomaly — question: if the business can safely support the preferred dividend burden, could it equally well have issued that amount as a bond at a lower net cost? test: compare the margin needed to cover bond interest plus preferred dividends combined against the margin for bond interest alone. evidence: high-grade industrial preferreds are almost always old, seasoned issues — companies strong enough to qualify at issuance nearly always chose to float bonds instead.
  > "We are led therefore, to the final conclusion that not only are sound preferred stocks exceptional but in a certain sense they must be called anomalies or mistakes, because they are preferred issues which should really be outstanding as bonds." @lines 8092-8095
- **title**: Liquidating value as a floor — question: what could owners actually realize by winding the business down and turning assets to cash, piecemeal? test: apply realization percentages by asset class (cash ~100%, receivables ~80%, inventory ~66⅔%, fixed/miscellaneous assets ~15%) and compare to market price. evidence: White Motor (1931) and Otis Company (1929) both sold well under liquidating value; both later paid stockholders far more than the market price via partial or full liquidation.
  > "By the liquidating value of an enterprise we mean the money that the owners could get out of it if they wanted to give it up." @lines 25578-25580
- **title**: Business reasoning vs. financial (Wall Street) reasoning — question: what would a private buyer pay for 100% of this enterprise, and does the market's aggregate valuation make sense on that basis? test: multiply market price per share by shares outstanding and ask whether that total is a price a prudent buyer would pay for the whole business. evidence: General Electric (1929-30) capitalized at ~$2.74 billion against ~$441 million of net assets — a premium few individual buyers ever priced explicitly.
  > "It is an almost unbelievable fact that Wall Street never asks, "How much is the business selling for?" Yet this should be the first question in considering a stock purchase." @lines 25316-25318
- **title**: Stockholder-management trusteeship (culture) — question: does management act as a trustee solely for the stockholders' interest, or does it exercise self-interest on compensation, buybacks, dividend timing, and disclosure? test: are departures from stockholders' interest specifically explained and put to a stockholder vote, or simply asserted as managerial prerogative? evidence: White Motor Company bought back stock for insiders' benefit at low prices while passing the common dividend that had been paid uninterrupted for fifteen years.
  > "the typical American stockholder is the most docile and apathetic animal in captivity." @lines 26735-26736

## Metrics
- **name**: Times fixed charges earned (bond/preferred coverage) — formula: net income available for fixed charges ÷ total fixed charges, computed on the "total-deductions" (over-all) basis covering every charge senior and junior alike, never the "prior-deductions" method that nets out only senior charges first; suggested minimums: public utilities 1½×, railroads 2×, industrials 3× for bonds (add preferred dividends, raise each bogey, for preferred stock). reading: higher = safer; the prior-deductions method is explicitly condemned as producing a "deceptively strong exhibit."
  > "Safety is measured not by specific lien or other contractual rights , but by the ability of the issuer to meet all of its obligations" @lines 4260-4261
- **name**: Working-capital (current) ratio — formula: current assets ÷ current liabilities. reading: 2-to-1 "was formerly regarded as a standard for industrial companies"; supplement with the "acid test" (current assets excluding inventory ÷ current liabilities ≥ 1). bench notes: falling below a peer-group average is not automatically disqualifying — penalizing the lower half of any group "by definition" is flagged as a logical fallacy.
  > "a minimum of $2 of current assets for $1 of current liabilities was formerly regarded as a standard for industrial companies." @lines 27577-27579
- **name**: Stock-value (equity-cushion) ratio — formula: market value of the junior capital (stock at market price) ÷ funded debt (bonds at par); used as a supplemental quantitative test of the going-concern value standing behind an industrial bond, since balance-sheet property values are unreliable. reading: a market-value equity cushion many times the debt is a strong assurance of bond safety; an exceedingly small one calls the bond's soundness into question.
  > "the market value of the stock issues is generally recognized as a better index of the fair going value of a business than is afforded by the balance-sheet figures or even the ordinary appraisal." @lines 7891-7893
- **name**: Earnings ratio / earnings yield — formula: annual earnings per share ÷ market price per share (the reciprocal of the price-earnings multiple); companion term "dividend ratio/yield" is dividend per share ÷ market price. reading: used throughout the book as the standard measure of what a purchaser is "paying" for a dollar of earning power.
  > "The earnings ratio, earnings return or earnings yield, is the ratio of the annual earnings to the market price" @lines 12298-12299
- **name**: Price-earnings multiplier ("quality coefficient") cap — formula: price = current per-share earnings × a quality coefficient reflecting dividend record, company standing, type of business, and general market temper. reading: Graham & Dodd's own ceiling — a multiplier above roughly 20× (an "earnings basis" under ~5%) carries an issue out of the investment price range entirely, regardless of how strong the qualitative story is.
  > "subject always to our principle that a multiplier higher than about 20" @lines 24283-24284
- **name**: Book value per share (tangible) — formula: (tangible assets − all liabilities and senior stock issues ahead of the common) ÷ common shares outstanding, deducting preferred stock at its "effective" (dividend-equivalent) par rather than a nominal stated par. reading: by 1940 no longer central to valuation and often wildly divergent from market price, but still worth a baseline check before buying or selling.
  > "suggest rather forcibly that the book value deserves at least a fleeting glance by the public before it buys or sells shares in a business undertaking." @lines 25493-25496

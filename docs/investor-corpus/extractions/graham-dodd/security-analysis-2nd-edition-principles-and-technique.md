## Question patterns

  > "The concept of earning power has a definite and important place in investment theory." @lines 20707-20708
- **pattern**: Have depreciation, depletion, or amortization charges been written down or eliminated for the purpose of inflating reported earnings, rather than to reflect the real economics of the assets consumed? — askWhen: an income statement shows a sudden earnings jump coinciding with a write-down of fixed assets, anchor: business-model
  > "excessive write-downs of fixed assets, for the avowed or obvious purpose of decreasing depreciation and increasing reported earnings, constitute an inexcusable subterfuge and should not be condoned by the accounting profession." @lines 16315-16318

## Search directives
- **directive**: When a company's reported per-share earnings jump sharply or diverge from peers, pull the balance sheet from the same and prior year-end and reconcile the change in surplus/net worth against the reported cumulative earnings less dividends; a persistent gap flags nonrecurrent items, subsidiary manipulation, or reserve charges routed around the income account — queryShapes: "[company] surplus account change vs reported earnings", "[company] balance sheet income account discrepancy", sourcePriority: primary financial statements (10-K/annual report, both years) over summary manuals or single-year income statements
  > "and the truer story told by the successive balance sheets" @lines 28718-28719
- **directive**: Before accepting a company's stated depreciation/depletion policy, cross-check it against the depreciation actually claimed on its federal income-tax return; a persistent gap between the tax-basis figure and the annual-report figure is a reliable tell of overstated reported earnings — queryShapes: "[company] tax return depreciation vs annual report", "[company] income tax basis depreciation discrepancy", sourcePriority: SEC filings / tax-basis disclosures over management's own annual-report presentation
  > "we now can advance no less than five major reasons for accepting, in general, the income tax figure rather than the income-account basis of depreciation." @lines 17967-17969
- **directive**: When a bond or preferred stock is being sold on "high coupon compensates for weaker security" logic, re-price it the way a common stock would be priced — via balance-sheet asset coverage, going-concern value, and earnings stability — since a fatter coupon cannot cure deficient safety — queryShapes: "[issuer] high-yield bond safety margin", "[issuer] business man's investment coupon rate", sourcePriority: original indenture/charter terms and issuer financials over secondary bond-guide summaries
  > "Deficient safety cannot be compensated for by an abnormally high coupon" @lines 4266-4267
- **directive**: Before crediting a multi-year earnings trend as a basis for valuation, separate it from the simple multi-year average and test whether the industry's or company's current condition (abnormally good or bad) is itself a corrective-forces candidate rather than a permanent state — queryShapes: "[industry] historical multi-decade earnings cycle reversion", "[industry] abnormal profitability corrective-forces precedent", sourcePriority: full-cycle industry data spanning at least one prior boom-bust rotation, not the most recent 3-5 years alone
  > "Abnormally good or abnormally bad conditions do not last forever." @lines 2264-2265
- **directive**: When a holding company, trust, or subsidiary-heavy issuer derives a large share of income from a fixed-value or annuity-like asset (bonds, long-term leases, royalties) buried inside an operating income statement, separately value that fixed-income component at an appropriate capitalization rate before applying an ordinary equity multiple to the balance — queryShapes: "[company] lease or royalty income as % of total earnings", "[company] embedded bond/annuity asset within operating income statement", sourcePriority: subsidiary/lease contract terms and the issuer's balance sheet detail over the consolidated income statement alone
  > "Since the American Tobacco Company was able to meet its obligation without- question, this annual rental income was equivalent to interest on a high-grade investment." @lines 24595-24597
- **directive**: When a corporate financial structure produces a persistent, glaring undervaluation relative to what a straightforward valuation of its parts would justify, treat that persistent discount itself as evidence the structure is wrong for stockholders, and search for precedent restructurings (special distributions, spin-offs, recapitalizations) that later closed an identical gap — queryShapes: "[company] persistent NAV discount structural cause", "[company] precedent restructuring closing valuation gap same industry", sourcePriority: corporate-action history of comparable companies with similar structural mismatches
  > "By means of these corporate rearrange¬ments the real values were speedily established in the market price." @lines 24662-24664

## Concepts
- **title**: Intrinsic value — question: what value is justified by the facts (assets, earnings, dividends, definite prospects) as distinct from the market's current quotation? test: does a "range of approximate value" clearly bracket the price above or below it? evidence: Wright Aeronautical (1922 vs. 1929) and J. I. Case (1933) show the range narrowing or widening with the certainty of the facts.
  > "it is a great mistake to imagine that intrinsic value is as definite and as determinable as is the market price." @lines 1298-1300
- **title**: Margin of safety — question: how large is the cushion of earning power or asset value above the fixed claims a purchase is subject to? test: coverage of fixed charges (and, for preferred stock, charges plus preferred dividends) against minimum benchmarks that scale with instability of the business. evidence: pyramided 1929 utility bonds that relied on "certain" future growth instead of a present margin collapsed when growth stopped.
  > "As the degree of instability increases, it must be offset by a greater margin of safety to make sure that interest charges will be met" @lines 4955-4957
- **title**: Earning power vs. simple average — question: does the year-by-year record show a "normal" or "modal" tendency, or is the multi-year average just an arithmetical resultant of disconnected figures? test: compare the spread of individual years around the average (S. H. Kress's tight cluster vs. Hudson Motors' wide scatter around similar averages). evidence: the qualitative stability of the underlying business, not the arithmetic, determines which case applies.
  > "The concept of earning power has a definite and important place in investment theory." @lines 20707-20708
- **title**: Optimum capitalization structure — question: does the company's mix of bonds, preferred, and common represent a suitable amount of leverage, or a speculative/top-heavy structure that throws every security in the capital stack into doubt? test: could the senior securities safely be issued and bought as investments on their own quantitative merits? evidence: hypothetical Companies A/B/C — value is destroyed, not created, once the senior layer exceeds what can safely be sold as an investment.
  > "The optimum capitalization structure for any enterprise includes senior securities to the extent that they may safely be issued and bought for investment." @lines 22752-22754
- **title**: Preferred-stock anomaly — question: if the business can safely support the preferred dividend burden, could it equally well have issued that amount as a bond at a lower net cost? test: compare the margin needed to cover bond interest plus preferred dividends combined against the margin for bond interest alone. evidence: high-grade industrial preferreds are almost always old, seasoned issues — companies strong enough to qualify at issuance nearly always chose to float bonds instead.
  > "We are led therefore, to the final conclusion that not only are sound preferred stocks exceptional but in a certain sense they must be called anomalies or mistakes, because they are preferred issues which should really be outstanding as bonds." @lines 8092-8095
- **title**: Liquidating value as a floor — question: what could owners actually realize by winding the business down and turning assets to cash, piecemeal? test: apply realization percentages by asset class (cash ~100%, receivables ~80%, inventory ~66⅔%, fixed/miscellaneous assets ~15%) and compare to market price. evidence: White Motor (1931) and Otis Company (1929) both sold well under liquidating value; both later paid stockholders far more than the market price via partial or full liquidation.
  > "By the liquidating value of an enterprise we mean the money that the owners could get out of it if they wanted to give it up." @lines 25578-25580
- **title**: Business reasoning vs. financial (Wall Street) reasoning — question: what would a private buyer pay for 100% of this enterprise, and does the market's aggregate valuation make sense on that basis? test: multiply market price per share by shares outstanding and ask whether that total is a price a prudent buyer would pay for the whole business. evidence: General Electric (1929-30) capitalized at ~$2.74 billion against ~$441 million of net assets — a premium few individual buyers ever priced explicitly.
  > "It is an almost unbelievable fact that Wall Street never asks, "How much is the business selling for?" Yet this should be the first question in considering a stock purchase." @lines 25316-25318
- **title**: Stockholder-management trusteeship (culture) — question: does management act as a trustee solely for the stockholders' interest, or does it exercise self-interest on compensation, buybacks, dividend timing, and disclosure? test: are departures from stockholders' interest specifically explained and put to a stockholder vote, or simply asserted as managerial prerogative? evidence: White Motor Company bought back stock for insiders' benefit at low prices while passing the common dividend that had been paid uninterrupted for fifteen years.
  > "the typical American stockholder is the most docile and apathetic animal in captivity." @lines 26735-26736
- **title**: Character of the enterprise vs. terms of the commitment — question: are analysts conflating the quality of the underlying business with the quality of the specific security's contractual terms, when the two dimensions are logically independent and both matter? test: score the enterprise's business quality and the issue's specific covenant/price terms separately, then compare the combined result against the price; evidence: Brooklyn Union Elevated 5s of 1950 (an "unattractive" traction industry with strong contractual protection) versus over-owned "blue chip" bonds priced purely on enterprise reputation, showing the popular preference for enterprise quality alone is "instinctively, rather than logically, right"
  > "Our distinction between the char¬acter of the enterprise and the terms of the commitment suggests a question as to which element is the more important." @lines 2047-2050
- **title**: Trend as a disguised, arbitrary prediction — question: does citing a rising or falling multi-year earnings trend actually strengthen a valuation's rigor, or does it substitute an unbounded, psychologically-driven forecast for a bounded, fact-based average? test: check whether the trend line has any principled stopping point for how many years forward it is projected, versus a stated average which inherently claims only to be "a rough index"; evidence: 1929 utility bond buyers who relied on continued earnings growth (rather than a present margin of safety) suffered when the trend reversed, even though railroad bonds valued on a static three-times-coverage average survived the same collapse
  > "A trend, however, cannot be used as a rough index; it represents a definite prediction of either better or poorer results, and it must be either right or wrong." @lines 2331-2333
- **title**: Reorganization and the shifting balance of power between bondholders and stockholders — question: under the pre-1933 receivership regime versus the Chandler Act/Trust Indenture Act regime, who effectively controls the outcome when a company defaults — the old insider management, or a genuinely disinterested party acting for creditors? test: check whether the reorganization plan was devised by a disinterested trustee (post-1933 rule) or by protective committees dominated by the original underwriting house and incumbent management (pre-1933 practice); evidence: Graham & Dodd's account of the shift from receiver-as-company-president toward the Chandler Act's disinterested-trustee-plus-SEC-review structure, explicitly designed to end "complete dominance of the situation in the hands of the old controlling group"
  > "More serious was the fact that the whole mechanics of reorganization tended to keep complete dominance of the situation in the hands of the old controlling group—who may have been inefficient or even dishonest, and who certainly had special interests to serve." @lines 9059-9063

## Metrics
- **name**: Times fixed charges earned (bond/preferred coverage) — formula: net income available for fixed charges ÷ total fixed charges, computed on the "total-deductions" (over-all) basis covering every charge senior and junior alike, never the "prior-deductions" method that nets out only senior charges first; suggested minimums: public utilities 1½×, railroads 2×, industrials 3× for bonds (add preferred dividends, raise each bogey, for preferred stock). reading: higher = safer; the prior-deductions method is explicitly condemned as producing a "deceptively strong exhibit."
  > "Safety is measured not by specific lien or other contractual rights , but by the ability of the issuer to meet all of its obligations" @lines 4260-4261
- **name**: Working-capital (current) ratio — formula: current assets ÷ current liabilities. reading: 2-to-1 "was formerly regarded as a standard for industrial companies"; supplement with the "acid test" (current assets excluding inventory ÷ current liabilities ≥ 1). bench notes: falling below a peer-group average is not automatically disqualifying — penalizing the lower half of any group "by definition" is flagged as a logical fallacy.
  > "a minimum of $2 of current assets for $1 of current liabilities was formerly regarded as a standard for industrial companies." @lines 27577-27579
- **name**: Stock-value (equity-cushion) ratio — formula: market value of the junior capital (stock at market price) ÷ funded debt (bonds at par); used as a supplemental quantitative test of the going-concern value standing behind an industrial bond, since balance-sheet property values are unreliable. reading: a market-value equity cushion many times the debt is a strong assurance of bond safety; an exceedingly small one calls the bond's soundness into question.
  > "the market value of the stock issues is generally recognized as a better index of the fair going value of a business than is afforded by the balance-sheet figures or even the ordinary appraisal." @lines 7891-7893
- **name**: Earnings ratio / earnings yield — formula: annual earnings per share ÷ market price per share (the reciprocal of the price-earnings multiple); companion term "dividend ratio/yield" is dividend per share ÷ market price. reading: used throughout the book as the standard measure of what a purchaser is "paying" for a dollar of earning power.
  > "The earnings ratio, earnings return or earnings yield, is the ratio of the annual earnings to the market price" @lines 12298-12299
- **name**: Price-earnings multiplier ("quality coefficient") cap — formula: price = current per-share earnings × a quality coefficient reflecting dividend record, company standing, type of business, and general market temper. reading: Graham & Dodd's own ceiling — a multiplier above roughly 20× (an "earnings basis" under ~5%) carries an issue out of the investment price range entirely, regardless of how strong the qualitative story is.
  > "subject always to our principle that a multiplier higher than about 20" @lines 24283-24284
- **name**: Book value per share (tangible) — formula: (tangible assets − all liabilities and senior stock issues ahead of the common) ÷ common shares outstanding, deducting preferred stock at its "effective" (dividend-equivalent) par rather than a nominal stated par. reading: by 1940 no longer central to valuation and often wildly divergent from market price, but still worth a baseline check before buying or selling.
  > "suggest rather forcibly that the book value deserves at least a fleeting glance by the public before it buys or sells shares in a business undertaking." @lines 25493-25496
- **name**: Book Value Short-Cut Formula — formula: Book Value per share of common = (Common Stock + Surplus Items − Intangibles) ÷ Number of shares outstanding, where "Surplus Items" includes premiums on capital stock and voluntary reserves (plant improvement, contingencies not known to be needed) but not reserves for known/probable liabilities. reading: worked example on U.S. Steel common, Dec. 31 1938 — tangible assets $1,711,000,000 less all liabilities/senior claims ahead of common ($688,000,000) = $1,023,000,000 net assets for common ÷ 8,700,000 shares = $117.59 book value per share. bench notes: distinguishing "voluntary" surplus-equivalent reserves from true liability reserves is the main judgment call in this formula.
  > "Book value per share (on 8,700,000 shares) $117.59" @lines 24888-24891
- **name**: Lease/Royalty Capitalization Discount Test — formula: compare the market's implied capitalization rate on a fixed, contractually-secured income stream (lease rental, bond interest held by a subsidiary) against the capitalization rate the same income would receive if held directly as a bond or lease by an ordinary investor; a persistent gap flags an undervalued corporate structure. reading: Lackawanna Securities' Glen Alden bond interest was being valued by the market at roughly a 10% capitalization rate (equivalent to pricing the underlying Glen Alden bonds at 37 cents on the dollar) versus their true high-grade-bond quality, showing the holding-company wrapper itself destroyed roughly two-thirds of the asset's recognized value. bench notes: the fix in all three of Graham & Dodd's worked examples (Northern Pipe Line, Lackawanna Securities, Tobacco Products) was a corporate rearrangement — special distribution, dissolution, or recapitalization — that "speedily established" real values in the market price.
  > "The price of 23 for a share of Lackawanna Securities was equiva¬lent to $60 face value of Glen Alden bonds at 37, plus $1 in cash" @lines 24523-24524

## Other

- **The book opens Chapter I by naming its own thesis as a deliberate departure from prevailing Wall Street habit** — before any technical material, Graham & Dodd frame the entire 700+ page treatise around the claim that Wall Street systematically underweights facts (balance sheets, earnings records) in favor of speculative narrative, setting up every subsequent chapter's worked examples as evidence for that founding claim.
  > "On numerous occasions prior to this point we have expressed our conviction that the balance sheet deserves more attention than Wall Street has been willing to accord it for many years past." @lines 24699-24702

- **A single hypothetical General Electric misvaluation recurs across the book as the canonical illustration of unsound "analysis"** — Graham & Dodd repeatedly invoke a hypothetical recommendation to buy General Electric common "at a price forty times its highest recorded earnings merely because of its excellent prospects" as the paradigm case of what a "thorough analysis" is explicitly not, using the same household-name blue chip to anchor the point that even the most reputable company can be a bad purchase at the wrong multiple.
  > "An "analysis" that recommended investment in General Electric common at a price forty times its highest recorded earnings merely because of its excellent prospects would be clearly ruled out, as devoid of all quality of thoroughness." @lines 3457-3460

- **The book documents, with dollar-and-cents precision, a real-time 40%-annualized arbitrage opportunity the authors themselves observed in General Electric Special preferred** — rather than a purely theoretical illustration, Graham & Dodd walk through an actual margin-financed trade (buy called-for-redemption preferred at 11, borrow against it at 2%, collect the $11 redemption plus dividend three months later) as a concrete case where a stock purchase on margin for a quick profit nonetheless met their strict definition of "investment" because the safety was assured by the redemption itself.
  > "This operation would have netted a sure return at the rate of 40% per annum on the capital invested" @lines 3535-3536

- **The book's account of the 1933-1939 bankruptcy-law overhaul reads as a piece of financial-regulatory history in its own right** — Graham & Dodd trace, chapter and verse, how the Chandler Act and the Trust Indenture Act of 1939 shifted reorganization power away from company-president-as-receiver and underwriter-controlled protective committees toward a disinterested trustee, the SEC, and court oversight, complete with named precedent cases (Radio-Keith-Orpheum, Fox Film, Kansas City Public Service, Baltimore and Ohio) — a level of institutional detail unusual for what is nominally a securities-valuation manual.

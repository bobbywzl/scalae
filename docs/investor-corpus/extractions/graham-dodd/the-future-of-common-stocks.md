## Concepts

- **title**: Book value approach to stock selection — test: a business worth its net asset value if prosperous and promising; evidence that buying at substantial discount from net worth could be attractive
  > "the value of the company as a private enterprise to a private owner, irrespective of market quotations for the shares. If the business has been prosperous, and is at least reasonably promising for tbe future, it should be worth its net asset value; bence an opportunity to buy an interest therein at a substantial discount from net worth could be considered attractive." @line 210-215

- **title**: Efficient market hypothesis limitations — question: does possessing complete information guarantee correct price setting if judgment is poor?
  > "The market may have had all the information it needed about Avon; what it has lacked is the right kind of judgment in evaluating its knowledge." @line 291-293

- **title**: Fixed Minimum Allocation Between Stocks and Bonds — question: should an investor's stock/bond split ever collapse entirely to one side, even under strong conviction?, test: hold a hard floor in both asset classes regardless of relative attractiveness, adjusting only the flexible middle portion, evidence: Graham prescribes a permanent 25% floor in each of bonds and equities (I am not proposing a 100 per cent stock position for any investor), with only the remaining half varied by relative attractiveness
  > "stock position for any investor. On the contrary, I think that everyone's total portfolio should always" @line 503-504

- **title**: Institutional Size and Analyst Numbers as No Guarantee of Rational Pricing — question: does adding more professional capital and more trained analysts to a market necessarily reduce mispricing, or can it coincide with wider swings?, test: compare pre- and post-institutionalization price amplitude for the same index; also check whether an institution's ownership trend tracks or contradicts the position's reported fundamentals, evidence: Graham reports wider, not narrower, fluctuation after institutions came to dominate market activity, contradicting the theoretical expectation of stabilization; the American Airlines case shows institutions increasing a stake through a deficit year into a 250x-earnings price ("this story hardly suggests that the institutions have been valiant contributors to 'efficient markets' and correct stock prices")
  > "amplitude of price fluctuations has, if anything, been wider than before the institutions came into tbe market on a grand scale." @line 450-452
  > "The company reported a deficit of $1.30 per share" @line 480

- **title**: Wall Street's Self-Inflicted Reputational Damage as a Distinct Risk Factor — question: is the greatest threat to future equity values external (economic, regulatory) or internal (the industry's own conduct)?, test: separate the industry's self-caused scandals/operational failures from genuine external economic threats when assessing what could depress future stock values, evidence: Graham explicitly ranks Wall Street's own ethics and operational failures above outside threats as a source of damage to equity values
  > "I insist that more damage has been done to stock values and to the future of equities from inside Wall Street than from outside Wall Street." @line 587-590

- **title**: Sound Reasoning Applied Past Its Valid Range ("Watershed Episodes") — question: does a conclusion that was correct at one price level remain correct after conditions have moved far past where it was formed?, test: re-test whether the original supporting facts (price level, bond yields, valuation gap) still hold before extending a past-derived conclusion to a new regime, evidence: Graham traces the 1920s bull market's justification to Smith's 1924 book, correct at Dow 90 but "blindly followed" all the way to 381
  > "Here was a striking example of the calamity that can ensue when reasoning that is entirely sound when applied to past conditions is blindly followed long after the relevant conditions have changed." @line 47-50

## Metrics

- **name**: Earnings/price ratio to bond yield relationship — formula as stated: one dollar of Dow earnings worth $17 at 4.4% bond yields should be worth only $8.80 at 8.5% bond yields; suggests stock earning yields should maintain ratio to bond rates
  > "If one dollar of Dow earnings were worth $17 when bond yields were 4.4 per cent, that one dollar is now worth only 52 per cent of $17, or $8.80, with AAA bonds at 8.5 per cent." @line 126-129

- **name**: Book value discount purchase target — formula: buy selected common stocks at two-thirds or less of book value, hold for sale at net asset value to achieve 50 percent profit
  > "the idea of buying selected common stocks—those meeting additional criteria of financial strength, etc.—obtainable at two-thirds or less of book value, and holding them for sale at their net asset value—to show a non-spectacular but quite satisfactory 50 per cent profit." @line 231-234

- **name**: Earnings-based stock valuation multiplier — formula: with 1973 normal earnings at $86, justified DJIA multiplier of nine suggests valuation of 775 based on AAA bond yields at 8.5 percent and historical earning multiples
  > "If you place those earnings at the record 1973 figure of $86, you arrive at a current valuation of only 775 for the DJIA." @line 133-134

- **name**: Required Earnings Yield as a Multiple of the Bond Rate — formula: required stock earnings yield = 4/3 × current AAA bond yield (e.g. 4/3 × 8.5% ≈ 11%), reading: cross-checked against the bond-ratio method, both converge on ~775 for the Dow, benchNotes: a second, independent derivation of the same target valuation — used as a consistency check rather than a standalone rule
  > "I should want the Dow or Standard and Poor's to return an earnings yield of at least four-thirds that on AAA bonds to give them competitive attractiveness with bond investments." @line 143-147

- **name**: Long-Run Growth Rate as a Third Independent Check — formula: 25-year historical annualized Dow growth (~4.5%) + assumed dividend yield (~5%) = expected total return (<10%), reading: this third method makes the 775 Dow valuation look "over generous" rather than conservative, so it functions as a downward-adjusting cross-check on the other two valuation methods, benchNotes: Graham explicitly triangulates three separate methods (bond-ratio, required-yield-multiple, historical-growth) rather than relying on one formula
  > "my calculations of growth rates over the past 25 years give an annual figure for the Dow of only 4-1/2 per cent. If this rate were to continue in the future, the expectable combination of growth plus dividends would produce less than a ten per cent overall return" @line 152-157

- **name**: Price-to-Book Multiple Ceiling for Growth-Stock Speculation — formula: observe ratio of price to book value for high-multiplier "Group I" growth issues; ratios above 5x, and some above 10x, are flagged as the threshold where price risk (not business weakness) turns the position speculative, reading: Graham draws the speculative line at price level relative to book, not at any flaw in the underlying company
  > "The trouble is, of course, that most of them sell at more than five times book value—and some more than ten times." @line 246-248

## Other

- **Descartes cited in French, then self-translated, as the article's epistemic thesis**: Graham reaches past finance entirely for his central claim against strong efficient-market theory — that having complete information is not the same as applying it well — grounding a 1974 markets argument in 17th-century philosophy of method.
  > "Ce n'est pas assez d'avoir l'esprit bon, mais le principal est de l'appiquer bien." @line 296-297

- **The American Airlines case study as a named, numbered indictment of institutional "efficiency"**: rather than a general claim, Graham walks through a specific holding — 2,000 canvassed institutions, a deficit year followed by two near-breakeven years, and a stock still bid to 250x earnings — as concrete evidence against the efficient-market and institutional-stabilization theses he is rebutting.
  > "The company reported a deficit of $1.30 per share" @line 480

- **Closing with Virgil rather than a summary paragraph**: Graham ends a technical valuation article not with a restated thesis but with a classical apostrophe borrowed from a building inscription, redirecting a farmer's blessing at "the common-stock buyers of this and future years" — a rhetorical signature of his late-career voice.
  > "O enviably fortunate Investors, if only you realized your current advantages!" @line 631-632

- **A rhetorical joke about the danger of too many analysts**: quoting Heinrich Heine's couplet about 45 German professors dooming the fatherland, Graham turns it on the profession itself — asking, half-seriously, what 11,000 competing security analysts chasing the same averages might do to market rationality.
  > "If only 45 professors can present such a menace, how about 11,000 analysts?" @line 439-440

## Concepts
- **title**: Price vs. Documented Value — question: Is the price basically supported by earnings, dividends, and asset value, or does it rest on sentiment alone?, test: The overvalued/cheap question is tested as two separate, explicit claims about the same price rather than one impression, evidence: Graham holds "not basically overvalued" and "not cheap" apart as distinct, independently-reasoned conclusions about the identical price level
  > "_ the impression that leading industrial stocks are not basically over- _ valued, but they are definitely not cheap ; and they are in danger of going over into an unduly high level." @line 95-98

- **title**: Special Situation — Event-Driven Mispricing — question: Does the value-realization catalyst come from a company-specific corporate event rather than a general market re-rating?, test: The security's expected value increase must trace to a named development in the company's own affairs, not to a hoped-for shift in market mood, evidence: Graham defines the category explicitly against ordinary undervaluation, requiring a probability-raising event tied to the company itself
  > "That is a security which upon study is believed to have a probability of increasing in value" @line 158-159

- **title**: Intrinsic Value as Private-Owner Value — question: Is "undervalued" defined against a market comparison or against what a private buyer of the whole enterprise would pay?, test: The valuation standard must reduce to what a rational private owner would pay for the entire business, not to a peer-multiple or historical-price comparison, evidence: Graham defines a security as undervalued when its price is "considerably less than the value of the company to a private owner," derived from balance sheet and income account study rather than market comparables
  > "in general can be defined as considerably less than the value of 'the company: to a private owner." @line 186-187

- **title**: Independence from Forecasting — question: Do the firm's results depend on correctly predicting the macro or market future, or are they structured to work regardless of it?, test: The stated method must not require forecasting skill to succeed, evidence: Graham states his business was built on the principle of making results independent of any view of the future, not on economic or market forecasting
  > "Mr. Grauam. As a matter of fact, I have never specialized in eco- nomic forecasting or market forecasting either. My own business has ! been largely based on the principle that if you can make your results: independent of any views as to the future you are that much better off. -" @line 1903-1906

- **title**: Confidentiality and No Self-Promotion — question: Does the organization avoid talking its own book, even after building a position?, test: No promotional activity toward third parties about held positions, before or after purchase, evidence: Graham states the firm keeps operations confidential even after acquiring shares and has never solicited buyers for its own holdings
  > "we have no interest in endeavoring to persuade people to buy stocks in which we have an ownership. We have never done it and we never will do it." @line 2003-2005

## Metrics
- **name**: Central Value of an Earnings Stream (Capitalized Multi-Year Average) — formula: Capitalize a 10-year average of earnings at roughly twice the prevailing high-grade (AAA) bond rate to estimate a "central value" for an index or stock (source badly OCR-garbled at the exact formula sentence; quoted verbatim below with artifacts preserved), reading: Applied to the Dow-Jones industrials at the start of 1955 the method yielded ~396 against an actual level only slightly higher — read as reassuring rather than alarming; in 1929 the method's 120 sat almost exactly at the geometric mean of the 381 high and the subsequent 41 low, benchNotes: Graham flags the method's own weak spot in the same breath — the 10 years used contained no real depression, so its "reasonableness" reading is conditional on continuing to avoid a severe business-cycle downturn

- **name**: Net Profit Margin, After-Tax vs. Pre-Tax Against a Distant Baseline — formula: Compare current after-tax margin to a multi-decade-prior after-tax margin, then separately compare pre-tax margins for the same two periods to isolate the tax-rate effect from any real operating change, reading: General Motors' after-tax margin now (~8.2%) sits well below the ~14-15% figure from decades earlier, but Graham attributes most of that gap to today's higher tax rates rather than operating decline — pre-tax, the two periods are "approximately the same, maybe somewhat less now", benchNotes: used to rebut a lack-of-competition inference drawn from a moving margin — a margin move that survives the pre-tax/after-tax split is a real operating signal, one that doesn't is a tax-policy artifact

  > "the margin of profit in and of itself is no larger now than it was in previous years." @line 1180-1181

- **name**: Dividend-vs-Retained-Earnings Market Weighting (Paid-Out Multiplier) — formula: Historically, compare the market-value effect of a dollar of paid-out earnings (dividends) against a dollar of retained earnings for the average stock, expressed as a ratio, reading: In the past a dollar of paid-out earnings carried roughly four times the market weight of a dollar of retained earnings for the average stock (long-term-growth stocks excepted); Graham judged this weighting was in active transition toward valuing retained earnings more highly, despite the tax code already favoring retention (single tax plus eventual capital-gains tax, versus two full taxes on distributed income) — investor behavior lagged the tax logic for decades, benchNotes: a market-sentiment metric, not a valuation input on its own — signals how much of a stock's re-rating potential depends on the market's current (and shifting) preference for payout policy rather than on the earnings themselves

- **name**: Earnings Multiplier Trend Against Flat Earnings (Sentiment-Isolation Metric) — formula: Track an index's price/earnings multiplier across successive years while confirming the underlying earnings figure is materially unchanged, isolating multiple expansion as the entire source of any price gain, reading: The Dow-Jones industrials' multiplier moved 8x (1948-50) → 10x (1953) → 14x (current, 1955) while index earnings were essentially flat since 1949 — Graham reads the entire 1953-55 price rise as a swing in investment sentiment ("from doubt to confidence"), not a change in business value, benchNotes: pairs with the Central Value metric above as a second, independent check — a market can look "about right" by the capitalized-earnings method while still showing a sentiment-driven multiplier climb that flags danger qualitatively

## Question patterns
- **pattern**: Is the current price basically fair, cheap, or dangerously extended relative to a documented estimate of value — and does the answer differ between a quantitative (price-to-earnings) check and a qualitative read of sentiment? — askWhen: initial valuation checkpoint and periodic re-rating of an existing position, anchor: business-model
  > "Quantitatively the market seems to be about right, but qualitatively I consider it to be on the high side and getting into a dangerous situation." @line 1086-1088

- **pattern**: Does management's incentive plan pay out only above a defined hurdle tied to what owners actually received, or does it pay regardless of shareholder outcomes? — askWhen: management-quality and compensation-structure diligence, anchor: culture
  > "We pay ourselves salaries on. the order of $25,000 and $15,000, and we also have a profit-sharing plan under which after a $40-a-share dividend is earned and paid in any year the management as a whole receives 20 percent of the additional amount earned and paid." @line 864-868

- **pattern**: How intense is rivalry among the company's closest peers, and has it visibly impaired a competitor's earning power — evidence of real competition rather than tacit, administered pricing? — askWhen: competitive-moat and pricing-power assessment, anchor: business-model
  > "I think the competition that existed between General Motors and Chrysler, for example, was very intense, and its effect on Chrysler was almost disastrous." @line 1371-1373

- **pattern**: What is the company's own standard for when information becomes material enough that it must reach all stockholders at once, rather than reaching only whoever is closest to management? — askWhen: governance and information-parity diligence, anchor: culture
  > "The basic point involved is that where there is a matter of major importance it is generally felt. that prompt disclosure should be made to all the stockholders so that nobody would get a substantial advantage in knowing that." @line 1514-1517

- **pattern**: Does the operator acknowledge a capacity ceiling — a scale past which more capital or volume would degrade results — or does its stated strategy implicitly assume unlimited scalability? — askWhen: strategy-capacity and growth-durability diligence, anchor: business-model
  > "The basic reason, Senator. has been that we have not believed that we could get the same satisfactory results on very large capital as we can get on a moderate amount of capital." @line 1062-1064

## Concepts
- **title**: Price vs. Documented Value — question: Is the price basically supported by earnings, dividends, and asset value, or does it rest on sentiment alone?, test: The overvalued/cheap question is tested as two separate, explicit claims about the same price rather than one impression, evidence: Graham holds "not basically overvalued" and "not cheap" apart as distinct, independently-reasoned conclusions about the identical price level
  > "leading industrial stocks are not basically overvalued, but they are definitely not cheap ; and they are in danger of going over into an unduly high level." @line 105-107

- **title**: Special Situation — Event-Driven Mispricing — question: Does the value-realization catalyst come from a company-specific corporate event rather than a general market re-rating?, test: The security's expected value increase must trace to a named development in the company's own affairs, not to a hoped-for shift in market mood, evidence: Graham defines the category explicitly against ordinary undervaluation, requiring a probability-raising event tied to the company itself
  > "That is a security which upon study is believed to have a probability of increasing in value" @line 168-170

- **title**: Independence from Forecasting — question: Do the firm's results depend on correctly predicting the macro or market future, or are they structured to work regardless of it?, test: The stated method must not require forecasting skill to succeed, evidence: Graham states his business was built on the principle of making results independent of any view of the future, not on economic or market forecasting
  > "I have never specialized in economic forecasting or market forecasting either. My own business has been largely based on the principle that if you can make your results independent of any views as to the future you are that much better off." @line 1945-1948

- **title**: Confidentiality and No Self-Promotion — question: Does the organization avoid talking its own book, even after building a position?, test: No promotional activity toward third parties about held positions, before or after purchase, evidence: Graham states the firm keeps operations confidential even after acquiring shares and has never solicited buyers for its own holdings
  > "we have no interest in endeavoring to persuade people to buy stocks in which we have an ownership. We have never done it and we never will do it." @line 2077-2079

- **title**: Non-Control, Valuation-Driven Ownership — question: Is a position built to profit from a mispricing, or to seize operating control?, test: Control-seeking should be the rare exception across a portfolio, not the default posture, evidence: out of roughly 400 companies invested in, Graham-Newman sought control in only 3 or 4
  > "out of about 400 companies that we may have invested in in the last few years, there would not be more than 3 or 4 in which we would have had any interest in acquiring control." @line 214-217

## Metrics
- **name**: Central Value of an Earnings Stream (Capitalized Multi-Year Average) — formula: Capitalize a 10-year average of earnings at roughly twice the prevailing high-grade (AAA) bond rate to estimate a "central value" for an index or stock (source badly OCR-garbled at the exact formula sentence; quoted verbatim below with artifacts preserved), reading: Applied to the Dow-Jones industrials at the start of 1955 the method yielded ~396 against an actual level only slightly higher — read as reassuring rather than alarming; in 1929 the method's 120 sat almost exactly at the geometric mean of the 381 high and the subsequent 41 low, benchNotes: Graham flags the method's own weak spot in the same breath — the 10 years used contained no real depression, so its "reasonableness" reading is conditional on continuing to avoid a severe business-cycle downturn
  > "have.found it useful to estimate the central value of the Dow-Jones industrial Y the simple method of capitalizing 10-year average earnings at twice St-.rate for high-grade bonds." @line 2188-2190
  > "found by this method in 1929 was 120, which happens to be about the geometrie mean between the high of 381 and the subsequent 1932 low of 41." @line 2206-2207

- **name**: Net Profit Margin, After-Tax vs. Pre-Tax Against a Distant Baseline — formula: Compare current after-tax margin to a multi-decade-prior after-tax margin, then separately compare pre-tax margins for the same two periods to isolate the tax-rate effect from any real operating change, reading: General Motors' after-tax margin now (~8.2%) sits well below the ~14-15% figure from decades earlier, but Graham attributes most of that gap to today's higher tax rates rather than operating decline — pre-tax, the two periods are "approximately the same, maybe somewhat less now", benchNotes: used to rebut a lack-of-competition inference drawn from a moving margin — a margin move that survives the pre-tax/after-tax split is a real operating signal, one that doesn't is a tax-policy artifact
  > "In the case of General Motors, for example, their profit margin last year, I think, after taxes was about 8.2 percent" @line 1303-1304
  > "the margin of profit in and of itself is no larger now than it was in previous years." @line 1300-1301

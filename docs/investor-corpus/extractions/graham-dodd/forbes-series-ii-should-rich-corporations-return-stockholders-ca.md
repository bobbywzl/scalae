## Concepts
- **title**: Ownership rights and reality — question: Do shareholders truly understand their fundamental legal and economic position as owners of the business?, test: Can a shareholder access the cash and assets they own through normal channels or company actions?, evidence: Shareholders fail to exercise ownership prerogatives, treating themselves as traders rather than owners
  > "If it only could be brought home to the millions of investors the country over, a long step would be taken in the direction of sounder corporate practices and a saner attitude towards stock values." @p.4
- **title**: Capital allocation imperatives — question: When a company accumulates surplus capital, what is the hierarchy of obligations to deploy it?, test: Does the company maintain business-required reserves, then return excess to owners rather than using it for ego projects or market manipulation?, evidence: Management must prioritize: maintain normal operations, sustain dividends, then return surplus
  > "The immediate result of such a movement would be to benefit the individual stockholder by placing funds in his hands to meet his urgent needs or to use as he sees fit." @p.2
- **title**: Market illusion vs. asset reality — question: Can speculation-driven valuations coexist with owner-oriented capital management?, test: Does the company act as though it serves shareholders or as though market sentiment is the only reality?, evidence: Earnings-obsessed valuation divorced from tangible asset backing
  > "In recent years financial writers have been unanimous in pointing out how unimportant are asset values as compared with earning power" @p.3
- **title**: Pro-rata repurchase as the ethically preferred cash-return mechanism — question: Among the available ways to return surplus cash (special dividend, par-value reduction, open-market buyback, pro-rata tender), which avoids conflicts of interest between remaining and departing owners?, test: Does the method treat all shareholders' claims on the surplus identically, or does it let insiders/directors selectively benefit one group?, evidence: Graham explicitly ranks pro-rata retirement above open-market purchases, citing the risk of paying too high a price or exploiting stockholders' necessities.
  > "Retirement of stock pro rata involves no conflict of interest between those selling out and those staying in; and it provides no opportunity for errors in judgment or unfair tactics on the part of the management." @p.3
- **title**: "Watering of earnings" as the accounting-era successor to "watering of assets" — question: Is a company's reported profit growth the product of real operations or of an arbitrary accounting choice designed to manufacture an earnings trend the market will pay a multiple on?, test: Compare pre- and post-adjustment earnings for changes in depreciation, goodwill treatment, or asset write-down policy that have no cash-flow counterpart., evidence: Graham draws the direct historical parallel between 1920s stock-watering (inflating asset books) and 1930s earnings-watering (deflating asset books to inflate earnings) as mirror-image frauds with the same underlying deception.
  > "In those days fixed assets were arbitrarily written up, in order to enlarge the book values, and thus facilitate a fictitious market price. In place of" @p.3-4
- **title**: Commercial-to-securities credit substitution as a systemic banking risk — question: When corporations no longer need bank credit (because they raised capital directly from stockholders) and stockholders instead borrow against their shares, does the banking system's collateral quality silently deteriorate?, test: Track the ratio of commercial loans to securities-collateralized loans across a banking cycle; a rising securities-loan share signals systemic fragility tied to stock-price volatility rather than business fundamentals., evidence: Federal Reserve Member Bank data showing commercial loans falling from $9.7B to $6.8B while securities loans rose from $7.5B to $12.5B, 1920-1932.
  > "The safety of the loans, and to some extent the solvency of the banks making" @p.2

## Metrics
- **name**: Net quick assets per share — formula: rapid conversion value of current assets minus current liabilities, divided by shares outstanding, reading: Graham identifies this as a floor or conservative value indicator; stock price should not fall far below this without investigation, bench notes: This metric prioritizes tangibility and liquidity over earnings multiples; presages the later net-net concept; resistant to accounting manipulation on asset side
  > "This price should be above the current market but in most cases below the net quick assets per share and therefore far below the book value." @p.2
- **name**: Cash holdings as percentage of business need — formula: total cash on hand divided by estimated normal working capital requirements for the business, reading: Surplus exists when cash exceeds what is necessary for normal operations, maintenance of facilities, and contingency; any excess should be returned to shareholders, bench notes: Unlike accounting definitions of working capital, this focuses on sufficiency for operations, not balance-sheet metrics; requires qualitative judgment on "normal" requirements
  > "Let corporations return to their stockholders the surplus cash holdings not needed for the normal conduct of their business." @p.2
- **name**: Earnings multiple inflation factor — formula: not mechanically stated but illustrated: earnings increase $1 per share (from $4 to $5), and price rises from 40 to 75, bench notes: Graham identifies arbitrary use of earnings multiples as a root cause of speculation; demonstrates how earnings emphasis without asset anchor enables valuation detachment
  > "A mere $1 increase in profits, from $4 to $5 per share, raised the value of a stock from 40 to 75" @p.3
- **name**: Repurchase tender price versus net quick assets (Hamilton Woolen example) — formula: tender price per share compared against net quick assets per share and the prior market price, reading: Hamilton Woolen's $65 tender to buy back one-sixth of outstanding shares was "about equal to the net quick assets and considerably above the previous market price," giving departing holders a fair asset-backed exit while returning capital raised in 1929, bench notes: Serves as a real-world benchmark for what a fair pro-rata repurchase price looks like relative to net quick assets.
  > "Recently Hamilton Woolen has offered to buy one-sixth of the outstanding shares pro rata at $65, which is about equal to the net quick assets and considerably above the previous market price" @p.2
- **name**: Cash-to-overdue-debt ratio in distressed reorganizations (Fisk Rubber example) — formula: cash on hand per $1,000 of overdue debt, alongside net quick assets excluding fixed assets, reading: Fisk Rubber held roughly $400 cash and nearly $900 in net quick assets per $1,000 of overdue debt, yet the reorganization plan offered creditors no cash at all — only stock in a new company, bench notes: Illustrates how reorganization terms can strip claimants of cash entitlements that the balance sheet clearly supports.
  > "The Fisk Rubber Co., for example, showed around $400 in cash on hand for each $1,000 of overdue debt, and nearly $900 in net quick assets, excluding the extensive factories, etc." @p.4

## Other
- **The "Treasurers Sleep Soundly While Stockholders Walk the Floor!" sidebar**: Graham (or his Forbes editor) closes the piece with a punchy call-and-response sidebar structured as catechism — a question about why the stockholder is poor, answered in a chain of short declaratives blaming forgotten asset value and forgotten ownership rights. It is a deliberately populist, almost sermon-like device distinct from the analytical prose above it.
  > "Why is the stockholder poor to-day? Because he borrowed from the banks in 1929 to put more cash into the companies he owns. Where is that cash now? Much of it is still held intact by his company." @p.4
- **"Alice in Wonderland financial logic"**: Graham reaches for Lewis Carroll to characterize the practice of writing fixed assets down to $1 purely to eliminate depreciation and inflate reported earnings — a vivid literary aphorism for accounting absurdity that recurs in his rhetorical style across the Forbes series.
  > "Since no one pays any attention to assets, why carry any assets on the books? This is another example of Alice in Wonderland financial logic." @p.3
- **Second installment of a named "spectacular series"**: Graham situates this piece explicitly as article two of a multi-part Forbes campaign on the "maladjustment between finances of corporations and their owners," with the editorial note promising a third installment — documenting the serialized, campaign-style journalism Graham conducted in 1932 during the depths of the Depression.
  > "FORBES presents herewith the second in this spectacular series of articles on the maladjustment between finances of corporations and their owners. The third article will appear next issue." @p.4
- **Named corporate case studies as evidence, not abstraction**: Rather than arguing in the abstract, Graham names specific companies — Simms Petroleum, Hamilton Woolen, Peerless Motors, Eureka Vacuum Cleaner, Standard Oil pipe lines, Bendix Aviation, Fisk Rubber, Prairie Pipe Line — each illustrating a different variant of the cash-hoarding or cash-return problem, giving the essay a documentary, almost prosecutorial texture typical of his Forbes journalism.
  > "A few corporations have followed this procedure, one of the earliest being Simms Petroleum. Recently Hamilton Woolen has offered to buy one-sixth of the outstanding shares pro rata at $65" @p.2

## Concepts
- **title**: Margin of Safety — question: Is the purchase price justified by documented intrinsic value with protective cushion?, test: Every stock purchase requires objective reasoning demonstrating value exceeds price with margin protecting investment, evidence: Graham requires explicit margin of safety in value terms as condition for any stock commitment
  > "he should be able to justify every purchase he makes and each price he pays by impersonal, objective reasoning that satisfies him that he is getting more than his money's worth for his purchase--in other words, that he has a margin of safety, in value terms, to protect his commitment" @line 61-64

- **title**: Investor Discipline vs Speculative Impulse — question: Does strategy rely on systematic principles or hope/fear/greed-driven price bets?, test: Consistent application of the same buying and selling rules regardless of market sentiment, evidence: Common stocks subject to irrational fluctuations from speculative impulses; disciplined investor distinguishes investing from speculation
  > "most of the time common stocks are subject to irrational and excessive price fluctuations in both directions, as the consequence of the ingrained tendency of most people to speculate or gamble--i.e., to give way to hope, fear and greed" @line 9-11

- **title**: Group Results vs Individual Stock Selection — question: Should portfolio construction focus on overall group performance or on picking individual outperformers?, test: Portfolio results driven by the aggregate performance of the group rather than expectations for any single issue, evidence: Graham explicitly advocates simplified approach relying on group results rather than individual stock expectations
  > "Essentially, a highly simplified one that applies a single criteria or perhaps two criteria to the price to assure that full value is present and that relies for its results on the performance of the portfolio as a whole--i.e., on the group results--rather than on the expectations for individual issues" @line 90-93

- **title**: Institutional Constraint in Security Selection — question: Do larger asset managers face structural disadvantages in accessing the best available opportunities?, test: Field of available securities accessible to institutions vs individual investors, evidence: Institutions confined to 300-400 largest corporations while individuals can access 3000+ listed issues
  > "Chiefly because these institutions have a relatively small field of common stocks to choose from--say 300 to 400 huge corporations--and they are constrained more or less to concentrate their research and decisions on this much over-analyzed group" @line 51-53

- **title**: Efficient-market concession for large, over-researched issues — question: Has the sheer volume of professional analytical coverage on major companies eliminated the excess-return opportunity that detailed fundamental research once offered, even though Graham himself pioneered that research approach?, test: Compare the historical record of detailed-analysis stock selection in a low-coverage era (pre-1950s) against a high-coverage era (1970s), and assess whether transaction/research costs now exceed the incremental edge from deeper analysis, evidence: Graham explicitly concedes he is "on the side of the 'efficient market' school of thought" for this narrow case — a striking reversal from the founder of fundamental security analysis, limited specifically to heavily-analyzed large caps rather than the broader market.

## Metrics
- **name**: Net-Current-Asset Value (Working Capital Value) — formula: Purchase stocks at less than current assets minus all liabilities (zero weight to plant and fixed assets), reading: Yielded approximately 20% per annum over 30-year period; foolproof systematic method based on group outcomes not individual results; reappeared viable after 1973-74 with 300+ issues available in 1976, benchNotes: Conservative balance-sheet floor metric; ignores asset quality and earning power; group-level statistical approach
  > "My first, more limited, technique confines itself to the purchase of common stocks at less than their working-capital value, or net-current-asset value, giving no weight to the plant and other fixed assets, and deducting all liabilities in full from the current assets" @line 105-107

- **name**: Price-to-Earnings Multiple (Graham's Primary Criterion) — formula: Seven times reported earnings for the past twelve months, reading: Consistently demonstrated 15% or better annual returns, twice the DJIA record over 1925-1975; exploits recurrent excessive optimism and apprehension of speculative public, benchNotes: Reported basis avoids quality adjustments; 50-year testing period confirms reliability; simplicity central to economic merit
  > "The criterion I prefer is seven times the reported earnings for the past 12 months. You can use others--such as a current dividend return above seven per cent or book value more than 120 percent of price, etc." @line 120-121

- **name**: Profit-objective and holding-period selling rule — formula: Set a target gain of 50-100% per purchase with a maximum holding period of 2-3 years; sell at market if the target isn't reached by the deadline, reading: A mechanical selling discipline paired with the buying discipline — removes emotional decision-making from the exit and forces portfolio turnover toward fresh opportunities rather than indefinitely holding underperformers, benchNotes: Applies specifically to Graham's systematic bargain-buying approach, not to permanent-holding "quality compounder" strategies; the fixed deadline is the operative mechanism, not the percentage target alone.
  > "he should set a reasonable profit objective on each purchase--say 50 to 100 per cent--and a maximum holding period for this objective to be realized--say, two to three years" @line 66-68

## Other

- **The "Vanity Fair" and "Falstaffian joke" characterization of Wall Street**: In one of his most quoted late-career lines, Graham gives an unusually harsh, literary verdict on the entire institution of the stock exchanges — invoking John Bunyan's Vanity Fair, Falstaff, and Shakespeare's "tale full of sound and fury, signifying nothing" — a striking rhetorical escalation from the measured, technical tone of his lectures decades earlier.
  > "The Stock Exchanges appear to me chiefly as a John Bunyan type of Vanity Fair, or a Falstaffian joke, that frequently degenerates into a madhouse--"a tale full of sound and fury, signifying nothing."" @line 13-15

- **The "huge laundry" metaphor for institutional trading**: Graham's memorable image of institutions "taking in large blocks of each other's washing" at a rate of 30 million shares a day "without true rhyme or reason" is a compact, quotable aphorism capturing his late-career skepticism toward the value-added of large-scale institutional trading activity.
  > "The stock market resembles a huge laundry in which institutions take in large blocks of each other's washing--nowadays to the tune of 30 million shares a day--without true rhyme or reason." @line 16-17

- **A blunt verdict that the financial community is well-intentioned but structurally miscast**: Graham draws a sharp distinction between the personal character of financial professionals (whom he calls "above average in intelligence, business honesty and sincerity") and their competence at the specific tasks Wall Street asks of them (forecasting), which he says most cannot do well despite trying "valiantly and ineffectively" — separating integrity from efficacy.
  > "They spend a large part of their time trying, valiantly and ineffectively, to do things they can't do well." @line 24-25

- **Graham reflects on his own textbook's declining relevance late in his career**: Speaking roughly forty years after "Graham and Dodd" (Security Analysis) was first published, Graham candidly states that the detailed-analysis approach that made him famous is no longer as rewarding an activity as it once was — a rare instance of the field's founding authority publicly revising his own life's methodological work in light of how the market had changed by 1976.
  > "This was a rewarding activity, say, 40 years ago, when our textbook "Graham and Dodd" was first published; but the situation has changed a great deal since then." @line 81-82

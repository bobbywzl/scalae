## Concepts

- **title**: Group-Based Quantitative Selection vs. Individual Company Analysis — question/test: Does disciplined application of simple quantitative criteria to groups of stocks outperform selective analysis of individual companies over long periods? Evidence: 50-year backtest on Moody's Industrial stocks, 2x Dow Jones outperformance
  > "I found the results were very good for 50 years. They certainly did twice as well as the Dow Jones. And so my enthusiasm has been transferred from the selective to the group approach." @line 203-206

- **title**: Maximum Capital Size as Constraint vs. Annual Rate of Return as Objective — question/test: Does limiting absolute capital to optimize per-dollar returns align better with long-term investing goals than growth for its own sake? Evidence: Deliberate Graham-Newman cap at $15M despite scalability
  > "The question of whether we could earn the maximum percentage per year was what interested us. It was not the question of total sums, but annual rates of return that we were able to accomplish." @line 135-138

- **title**: Market Price Inefficiency Despite Information Dispersion — question/test: Can widely distributed information still result in irrational pricing? Evidence: Practical Wall Street observation contradicting efficient market claims
  > "The idea of saying that the fact that the information is so widely spread that the resulting prices are logical prices-that is all wrong. I don't see how you can say that the prices made in Wall Street are the right prices in any intelligent definition of what right prices would be." @line 261-265

- **title**: Independence of Thought as Essential vs. Consensus Following — question/test: Is contrarian thinking a prerequisite for investment success or merely advantageous? Evidence: Graham-Newman never followed crowd consensus; consistent outperformance
  > "We never followed the crowd, and I think that's favorable for the young analyst." @line 371-372

- **title**: Optimal fund size as a rate-of-return constraint, not a growth objective — question: Should an investment firm deliberately cap its assets under management to preserve the ability to earn superior percentage returns, even when it could raise and deploy far more capital?, test: Compare realized annual percentage returns of firms that cap AUM against those that scale AUM aggressively over the same market opportunities, and assess whether opportunity scarcity (limited high-conviction bargains) binds before capital does, evidence: Graham-Newman deliberately capped itself at $15 million — "only a drop in the bucket these days" by 1976 standards — prioritizing "the maximum percentage per year" over "total sums."
  > "We could have built up an enormous business had we wanted to, but we limited ourselves to a maximum of $15 million of capital" @line 132-134

- **title**: Persistent, cyclical, unlearnable market psychology ("the Bourbons" of Wall Street) — question: Does the Wall Street community as a body ever genuinely internalize the lessons of past speculative excesses (Go-Go funds, growth cult, two-tier market), or does each cycle repeat the same psychological pattern of greed, fear, and hope regardless of prior experience?, test: Compare the specific behavioral patterns (over-optimism, over-pessimism, chasing narratives) across multiple historical market cycles the observer has personally witnessed, and check whether institutional "lessons learned" claims are contradicted by the recurrence of the same behavior, evidence: Graham draws the explicit "Bourbons" analogy (forgot nothing, learned nothing — inverted to "learn nothing, forget everything") and predicts with confidence that 1970 and 1973-74-style episodes "will be duplicated in the next five years," treating this as a near-certain feature of human nature rather than a correctable market flaw.
  > "I have no confidence whatever in the future behavior of the Wall Street people. I think this business of greed-the excessive hopes and fears and so on-will be with us as long as there will be people." @line 404-407

## Metrics

- **name**: Net Working Capital Discount — formula: Buy stocks trading at two-thirds of net working capital per share (approximately 33% margin of safety), replicated across 30-company portfolios
  > "If a stock with $50 working capital sells at $32, that would be an interesting stock. If you buy 30 companies of that sort, you're bound to make money. You can't lose when you do that." @line 274-277

- **name**: Earnings Yield to AAA Bond Yield Multiple — formula: Earnings yield at minimum double the prevailing AAA bond interest rate; enforce maximum P/E multiples of 10x when rates under 5%, 7x when rates exceed 7%
  > "Basically, I want to double the interest rate in terms of earnings return. However, in most years the interest rate was less than five percent on AAA bonds. Consequently, I have set two limits. A maximum multiple of 10 even when interest rates are under five percent, and a maximum multiple of 7 times even when interest rates are above seven percent as they are now." @line 228-233

- **name**: Dow Jones Outperformance Benchmark — formula: 50-year study of group-based quantitative methods on all Moody's Industrial stocks achieved approximately 2x the Dow Jones return
  > "I found the results were very good for 50 years. They certainly did twice as well as the Dow Jones." @line 203-204

- **name**: Closed-end fund discount capture — formula: Buy closed-end investment company shares trading at an average discount of approximately 15% to net asset value; dollar-cost-average via periodic fixed-dollar purchases, reading: A simple, low-effort entry strategy Graham personally recommended to a family member starting out in the business — buying a diversified basket "at start ahead of the game" purely from the structural discount to NAV, independent of stock-picking skill, benchNotes: A beginner-friendly, low-maintenance variant of the group-bargain approach; explicitly framed by Graham as sufficient for someone with no track record yet to "start on a sound basis."
  > "You can buy closed-end investment companies at 15 percent discounts on an average. Get your friends to put "x" amount of dollars a month in these closed-end companies at discounts and you will start ahead of the game and you will make out all right." @line 379-383

## Other

- **The GEICO origin story — a $720,000 stake that became worth over a billion dollars**: Graham recounts, in his own words, how Graham-Newman bought half of GEICO for $720,000 and that stake was later worth "over a billion dollars in the stock market" — one of the most famous single investment outcomes in his career, told here directly by Graham himself as a matter-of-fact recollection rather than as a triumphant boast, immediately followed by candid concern about GEICO's subsequent large losses.
  > "We bought half the company for $720,000. It turned out later that we were worth-the whole company--over a billion dollars in the stock market. This was a very extraordinary thing." @line 19-22

- **Personal financial history through the 1929 crash — leveraged despite good judgment on price**: Graham discloses that despite correctly judging in 1929 that "prices were too high" and avoiding speculative favorites, he had borrowed money — "which was a mistake" — and had to "sweat through the period 1929-1932" as a direct result, a rare first-person admission of a specific personal financial mistake (leverage) distinct from a stock-picking error.
  > "All I knew was that prices were too high. I stayed away from the speculative favorites. I felt I had good investments. But lowed money, which was a mistake, and I had to sweat through the period 1929-1932. I didn't repeat that error after that." @line 66-70

- **Security Analysis and a failed Broadway play opened in the same year**: In an unusual and little-known biographical detail, Graham reveals that the first edition of Security Analysis (1934) was published the same year as a play he wrote, "Baby Pompadour" (also titled "True to the Marines"), which was produced on Broadway and closed after only one week — a striking juxtaposition of his greatest professional triumph and a personal creative failure in the same year.
  > "Yes. "Baby Pompadour" or "True to the Marines." It was produced twice under two titles. It was not successful. Fortunately, Security Analysis was much more successful." @line 166-168

- **Deciding to retire in 1956 because the work no longer challenged him**: Graham explains his own retirement from active management not in terms of financial success or age, but explicitly because the firm's approach had become so refined that it "no longer presented any basic problems to be solved" and remaining tasks were merely "repetitions of old problems which I found no special interest in solving" — an unusually candid account of losing intellectual interest as the real driver of his career decision.
  > "We were no longer very challenged after 1950. About 1956, I decided to quit and to come out here to California to live." @line 119-121

- **Practical mentorship of a nephew starting out in Wall Street**: Graham recounts giving his nephew Dick concrete, actionable starting advice (buying discounted closed-end funds via dollar-cost averaging) rather than abstract principles, and notes with a trace of wistfulness that once his nephew succeeded and "the big bull market came along," he moved into "an enormous amount of speculative business" — a small personal story revealing both Graham's mentoring style and his ambivalence about how even well-grounded beginners drift toward speculation.
  > "I had a nephew who started in Wall Street a number of years ago and came to me for some advice." @line 377-378

## Concepts

- **title**: Contrarian timing — buy despair, sell euphoria — question/evidence: when does crowd sentiment reach extremes?
  > "To buy when others are despondently selling and to sell when others are greedily buying requires the greatest fortitude, even while offering the greatest reward." @line 50

- **title**: Divergence principle (prices vs values) — why index passivity fails — evidence: prices oscillate wider than fundamentals justify
  > "Share prices fluctuate much more widely than values. Therefore, index funds will never produce the best total return performance." @line 64

- **title**: Short-Term Owner Exhaustion as a Timing Signal — test: has the flow of short-term sellers (or buyers) genuinely dried up, rather than merely paused?, evidence: framed as the actual trigger point for both buying and selling, distinct from valuation alone
  > "The time to buy a stock is when the short-term owners have finished their selling, and the time to sell a stock is often when short-term owners have finished their buying." @line 62

- **title**: Popularity Decay of Selection Methods — test: has this stock-picking or market-timing method become popular enough that its crowding has begun eroding its edge?, evidence: explicit instruction to rotate away from popular methods toward unpopular ones
  > "When any method for selecting stocks becomes popular, then switch to unpopular methods. As has been suggested in Maxim 3, too many investors can spoil any share-selection method or any market-timing formula." @line 74

- **title**: Individual Decision-Maker Superiority Over Committees — test: is investment selection ultimately vested in one accountable person rather than diffused across a group?, evidence: stated flatly as a maxim, distinct from the process/valuation maxims around it
  > "The best performance is produced by a person, not a committee." @line 80

- **title**: Asymmetric Payoff From Deep-Distress Bargains — test: does the position size stay small (limiting downside to the premium paid) while the potential multiple on survival is very large?, evidence: the WWII $100-into-$4,000 (40x) Missouri-Pacific trade

## Other

- **The 1939 WWII trade as origin myth of "maximum pessimism"**: Told in Templeton's own words from a 1997 Charlie Rose interview, this is the single most concrete illustration in the corpus of "buy at the point of maximum pessimism" — buying $100 worth of every stock trading below a dollar (including 37 already bankrupt) days after Hitler invaded Poland, reasoning that a world war would revive even worthless companies, and turning it into a 40x return on the standout position.
  > "I looked over all the New York Stock Exchange and the American Stock Exchange. There were 104 companies whose prices had gone down below a dollar a share. And so I called up Dick Platt and said, "Buy me $100 worth of everything below a dollar a share" and he did what you just said" @line 26

- **A Rhodes Scholar funded by poker winnings**: The brief biographical aside — that young Templeton traveled the world on 90 pounds won playing poker at Oxford — is a small but telling detail about the risk-tolerant, opportunistic character that later expressed itself as a professional discipline of buying what others feared.
  > "But before all that, he was a Yale graduate, a Rhodes Scholar, and a world traveler (on 90 pounds of poker winnings at Oxford)." @line 20

- **Prayer as the final maxim, deliberately placed last**: Of the 22 numbered maxims — mostly technical rules about valuation, diversification, and contrarian timing — the very last is explicitly spiritual, framing clear thinking and fewer "stupid mistakes" as a downstream benefit of beginning with prayer, a closing note that mirrors the same placement pattern in "16 Rules for Investment Success."
  > "If you begin with prayer, you can think more clearly and make fewer stupid mistakes." @line 82

- **The 2001 dot-com short as evidence that contrarian character survived into old age**: Nearly ninety years old and decades after his most famous bargain-hunting years, Templeton is shown running a disciplined, rules-based short book against 84 overpriced Nasdaq IPOs timed to insider lock-up expirations — proof that his willingness to act against the crowd, and his systematic risk management around timing catalysts, persisted across his entire career.
  > "I gave Merrill Lynch an order to short 84 Nasdaq stocks which were a sizable tech IPO selling at three times the price set in the IPO and where insder holdings were locked up so insiders couldn't sell." @line 32

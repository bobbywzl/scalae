## Concepts

- **title**: Real Return as Sole Rational Objective — test: does investment strategy account for taxes and inflation impact?, evidence: purchasing power preservation over nominal gains
  > "This means the return on invested dollars after taxes and after inflation. This is the only rational objective for most long-term investors." @line 50-52

- **title**: Investing vs. Trading/Speculating — test: is the holding period and reasoning grounded in business value or in short-term price movement?, evidence: casino framing of frequent in-and-out trading, options, short-selling, futures
  > "The stock market is not a casino, but if you move in and out of stocks every time they move a point or two, or if you continually sell short…or deal only in options…or trade in futures…the market will be your casino." @line 66-69

- **title**: Maximum Pessimism as Buy Signal — test: when is consensus belief most bearish vs. actual business fundamentals?, evidence: historical outperformance when buying against sentiment
  > "Buy when most people… including experts…are pessimistic, and sell when they are actively optimistic." @line 143-144

- **title**: Quality as Multi-Dimensional Moat — test: does the company exhibit sustained competitive advantage across multiple dimensions?, evidence: market leadership, management durability, capitalization for growth
  > "Quality is a company strongly entrenched as the sales leader in a growing market. Quality is a company that's the technological leader in a field that depends on technical innovation." @line 150-152

- **title**: Stock Market as Collection of Individual Valuations, Not Macro Proxy — test: is the company's valuation independent of market trend?, evidence: individual stocks can move counter to market
  > "A wise investor knows that the stock market is really a market of stocks. While individual stocks may be pulled along momentarily by a strong bull market, ultimately it is the individual stocks that determine the market, not vice versa." @line 163-165

- **title**: Diversification Along Three Axes (Industry, Risk, Country) — test: is exposure spread across industries, risk profiles, and national markets rather than concentrated in one?, evidence: worldwide search widens the bargain set beyond any single nation
  > "So you diversify—by industry, by risk and by country. For example, if you search worldwide, you will find more bargains—and possibly better bargains—than in any single nation." @line 186-187

- **title**: Buy-and-Forget Is a Myth — test: is the portfolio being actively re-examined as conditions change, not left on autopilot?, evidence: no bull or bear market is permanent, and no stock can be bought and forgotten
  > "No bull market is permanent. No bear market is permanent. And there are no stocks that you can buy and forget." @line 215-217

- **title**: Post-Crash Re-Underwriting Test — test: after a panic, would this position be bought fresh at today's price?, evidence: the answer, not inertia, should determine whether to hold or sell
  > "If you didn't own these stocks now, would you buy them after the market crash? Chances are you would. So the only reason to sell them now is to buy other, more attractive stocks." @line 237-239

- **title**: Learning From Mistakes as a Competitive Advantage — test: does the investor treat errors as a diagnostic input rather than a source of shame or bigger risk-taking?, evidence: forgiveness of self, avoidance of "this time is different" thinking
  > "The big difference between those who are successful and those who are not is that successful people learn from their mistakes and the mistakes of others." @line 252-254

- **title**: Epistemic Humility ("Doesn't Understand All the Questions") — test: is conviction paired with active questioning rather than certainty?, evidence: a cocksure approach precedes disappointment or disaster
  > "A cocksure approach to investing will lead, probably sooner than later, to disappointment if not outright disaster." @line 285-286

- **title**: No Free Lunch (Sentiment, IPO Commissions, Tips) — test: is the buy decision grounded in valuation rather than nostalgia, "saving" a commission, or inside information?, evidence: sentimental favorites, IPO pricing structure, and hot tips all mislead
  > "Never invest on sentiment. The company that gave you your first job, or built the first car you ever owned, or sponsored a favorite television show of long ago may be a fine company. But that doesn't mean its stock is a fine investment." @line 296-299

- **title**: Structural Long-Term Optimism — test: is the base-rate expectation for equities positive absent an era-specific catastrophe?, evidence: even the "dark '70s" saw many managers and individual investors make money in stocks
  > "For 100 years optimists have carried the day in US stocks. Even in the dark '70s, many professional money managers—and many individual investors too—made money in stocks, especially those of smaller companies." @line 318-321

- **title**: Relaxed Long-Term Ownership Beats Active Switching — test: is turnover driven by conviction changes or by restlessness?, evidence: Lucien Hooper's observation that relaxed owners outperform traders who switch inventory
  > "is how much better the relaxed, long-term owners of stock do with their portfolios than the traders do with their switching of inventory." @line 74-76

## Metrics

- **name**: Real Purchasing Power Impact of Inflation — formula: purchasing power retention over decade under inflation regime (e.g., 4% inflation = 32% loss of buying power in 10 years; portfolio must grow 47% to remain even), reading: 4% inflation reduces $100,000 to $68,000 buying power in 10 years; to preserve value requires $147,000 (47% nominal gain)
  > "If inflation averages 4%, it will reduce the buying power of a $100,000 portfolio to $68,000 in just 10 years. In other words, to maintain the same buying power, that portfolio would have to grow to $147,000—a 47% gain simply to remain even over a decade." @line 60-62

- **name**: Historical Stock Outperformance Over Alternatives — formula: compare annualized returns of S&P 500 (12.5%, 1950s-1980s) vs. inflation (4.3%), Treasury bills (5.2%), corporate bonds (5.4%), reading: stocks outperformed inflation in all four decades except the '70s, outperformed bonds in every decade
  > "From the start of the 1950s through the end of the 1980s—four decades altogether—the S&P 500 rose at an average rate of 12.5%, compared with 4.3% for inflation, 4.8% for US Treasury bonds, 5.2% for Treasury bills, and 5.4% for high-grade corporate bonds." @line 104-108

- **name**: Index Turnover as Competitive Decay Signal — formula: track percentage of index constituents exiting (acquisition, bankruptcy, privatization, decline) as indicator of sectoral disruption, reading: 33% of Dow 30 changed in 12-year period (1978-1990); 30 of Fortune 100 largest dropped in 7 years (1983-1990)
  > "From 1978 through 1990, one of every three issues changed—because the company was in decline, or was acquired, or went private, or went bankrupt." @line 222-223

- **name**: Dow Jones Industrials Long-Term Growth — formula: Dow appreciation plus reinvested dividends over 45.5-year period, reading: 11.4% annualized from January 1946 through June 1991 vs. 4.4% inflation
  > "From January of 1946 through June of 1991, the Dow Jones Industrial Average rose by 11.4% average annually—including reinvestment of dividends but not counting taxes—compared with an average annual inflation rate of 4.4%." @line 98-100

- **name**: Single-Day Crash Magnitude (1987) — formula: single-session portfolio drawdown experienced in the October 1987 crash, reading: a 15% loss in a single day is cited as the scale of shock to withstand without panic-selling
  > "Sometimes you won't have sold when everyone else is buying, and you'll be caught in a market crash such as we had in 1987. There you are, facing a 15% loss in a single day. Maybe more." @line 232-234

- **name**: Long-Range Dow Forecast (Templeton, 1993) — formula: forward-looking index level target based on continuation of historical growth trend, reading: at least an even chance the Dow reaches 6,000 by the start of the 21st century (from roughly 3,000+ in 1993)
  > "By the time the 21st century begins—it's just around the corner, you know—I think there is at least an even chance that the Dow Jones Industrials may have reached 6,000, perhaps more." @line 331-333

## Other

- **Begin with a prayer**: The most explicitly spiritual of the sixteen rules, placed immediately after "learn from your mistakes" — a discipline of clear thinking rooted in humility rather than a superstition, characteristic of Templeton's lifelong fusion of faith and investing.
  > "If you begin with a prayer, you can think more clearly and make fewer mistakes." @line 258-259

- **The Will Rogers frame**: Templeton opens the entire piece not with a market statistic but with a folksy moral — casting speculation as gambling and investing as its disciplined opposite, setting the character-first tone that runs through his fifty years of practice.
  > "I can sum up my message by reminding you of Will Rogers' famous advice." @line 25-27

- **Global bargain-hunting as identity**: The instruction to diversify "by country" and "search worldwide" is not a throwaway aside but the founding insight of Templeton's career — he built his reputation buying unloved, undervalued markets outside the US decades before that was common practice.
  > "So you diversify—by industry, by risk and by country. For example, if you search worldwide, you will find more bargains—and possibly better bargains—than in any single nation." @line 186-187

- **Structural optimism about the human future**: Closing the piece, Templeton ties stock-market optimism to geopolitical hope — the end of the Cold War as a reason wealth and trade would compound — reflecting his broader philosophy that free markets and free people tend toward progress over long spans of time.
  > "With the fall of communism and the sharply reduced threat of nuclear war, it appears that the US and some form of an economically united Europe may be about to enter the most glorious period in their history." @line 324-326

## Search directives

- **directive**: Study operator-run businesses by reading widely, talking directly to CEOs and business operators, and examining annual reports and major publications — queryShapes: "Where can credible business intelligence originate?", "What sources reveal management quality and business durability?", sourcePriority: direct operator conversation > annual reports > major publications > investor peers
  > "Ideas come to me from all sources, principally from reading and talking. I don't discriminate how they come, as long as they are good ideas." @p.28

- **directive**: Identify businesses with engineering cultures capable of adapting to technological change and solving complex problems — queryShapes: "What signals predict resilience under disruption?", "How do we evaluate organizational adaptability?", sourcePriority: business track record > management culture indicators > market position stability
  > "Successful companies are able to deal with change consistently by hiring the right people, building the right culture, and staying ahead of their competitors." @p.31

- **directive**: Distinguish which macro trends require understanding versus which are neutral to investment decision — queryShapes: "Is this macro factor a tailwind or headwind to my position?", "Do I actually need to predict this outcome?", sourcePriority: portfolio impact assessment > personal competence admission
  > "I pay attention to those macro trends only in the hope that I can have comfort that they're a tailwind as opposed to a headwind." @p.34

- **directive**: Verify a founder-led company's competitive record against a genuinely open, uncoddled market (no protected home-field advantage) before crediting the founder's engineering culture — queryShapes: "<COMPANY> home market advantage vs open competition", "<COMPANY> market share growth vs. established international rivals", "<COMPANY> founder engineering background"; sourcePriority: verified unit sales/market-share trajectory over management narrative
  > "They never had any home advantage whatsoever because China's auto market started out completely open with everybody competing." @p.27-28

- **directive**: Screen out businesses whose short-selling case rests on an eventual fraud unwinding, since fraud can be sustained a long time and borrowing costs/margin calls can bankrupt a correct short before the thesis plays out — queryShapes: "<COMPANY> short interest and borrow cost", "<COMPANY> fraud allegation timeline vs stock price", sourcePriority: position-survivability math (unlimited downside, capped upside) over thesis correctness alone
  > "On the long side, you have 100% downside but unlimited upside. On the short side, you have 100% upside and unlimited downside. I do not like that math." @p.26

- **directive**: When gauging a China-exposed real-estate or urbanization thesis, corroborate anecdotal "ghost town" evidence against contradicting evidence of fully-occupied, formerly-empty districts (e.g., Pudong's history) before drawing a bubble conclusion — queryShapes: "<CITY/REGION> vacancy rate high-rise occupancy trend", "<REGION> historical ghost town later occupied", sourcePriority: longitudinal occupancy data over a single point-in-time observation
  > "I remember, twenty years ago that Pudong was viewed as a semi-ghost town. Today, you cannot help but be impressed by the economic vibrancy there." @p.33

## Concepts

- **title**: Intellectual honesty as foundation for investment edge — defined as distinct competency buckets
  > "Investing is about intellectual honesty. You want to know what you know. You want to know, mostly, what you don't know. If understanding the management team is not in the cards, it's not in the cards." @p.29

- **title**: Circle of competence as dynamic and expandable through business study — shaped by personal interest rather than predetermined boundaries
  > "I let my own personal interests define my circle of competence. Obviously I know something about China, Asia, and America – those are things that I am really familiar with." @p.25

- **title**: Value investing as binary inoculation not gradual adoption — either the principle clicks immediately or not at all
  > "There are few people that switch in between or get it gradually. They either get it right away or they don't get it at all." @p.24

- **title**: Investing as zero-sum competitive game requiring informational and analytical superiority
  > "You really want to be sure that you are better informed and better reasoned than the person on the other side of the trade." @p.25

- **title**: The four-way intellectual-honesty taxonomy — know/don't-know/don't-have-to-know/unknown-unknowns as the operative test under stress
  > "The most important thing in our business is intellectual honesty. What I mean is four different things: know what you know, know what you don't know, know what you don't have to know, and realize that there is always a possibility that "you don't know that you don't know."" @p.32

- **title**: Three-bucket idea triage (yes / no / too hard) — most ideas are correctly discarded as unanalyzable rather than forced into a judgment
  > "There are basically three buckets that Charlie has. "Yes", "no", or "too hard". Most of the things fall in "too hard."" @p.30

- **title**: Short-selling asymmetry as structurally unattractive regardless of thesis correctness — capped upside, unlimited downside, borrow costs and mind-consuming distraction combine to make shorting a poor discipline even when directionally right
  > "That's why I could be 100% right and bankrupt at the same time. But, you know what, you go bankrupt first!" @p.26

- **title**: Win-win value creation as a preferred mode over pure zero-sum securities trading — a shift from cheap-securities hunting toward building/backing businesses (venture investing) where all sides gain
  > "I'm more interested, by my nature, in win-win situations. I want to create wealth together with the business operators and employees when I invest." @p.26

- **title**: Engineering/adaptive culture as the durable predictor of survival under rapid technological change — not the specific technology itself
  > "Successful technology companies are the ones that are capable of reinventing themselves and dealing with change. Take the example of Intel. The whole business changes every 18 months." @p.30-31

- **title**: The three legitimate sell triggers — proven mistake, valuation euphoria, or a strictly better alternative — as the complete decision framework for exiting a position
  > "One should make sell decisions on one of three occasions. Number one, if you make a mistake, sell as fast as you can, even if it's a correct mistake." @p.31

- **title**: Crisis as an intellectual-honesty stress test — market panics reveal which investors had actually sorted their knowledge into the four honesty categories beforehand, rather than creating new information
  > "That's why people freeze in the midst of a crisis. People freeze because they were not intellectually honest before." @p.32

## Metrics

- **name**: Himalaya Capital compensation structure — formula adopted from original Buffett partnership model
  - No management fee charged to investors
  - 6% annual return provided to investors at no cost (hurdle rate)
  - 25% of returns above 6% taken as general partner carry
  - 100% of general partner capital invested in fund (no separate account management)
  > "We don't take any management fee. We provide a 6% return for free to our investors and then take 25% after that. I don't invest anything outside of the fund. I put all of my investment capital into my funds. So it's a true partnership." @p.29

- **name**: Three occasions for sell decisions — framework for portfolio adjustment discipline
  1. When analysis is proven wrong (even "correct mistakes" where low-probability event occurs)
  2. When valuation swings to extreme euphoria or pessimism
  3. When discovering materially better opportunity to improve portfolio
  > "One should make sell decisions on one of three occasions. Number one, if you make a mistake, sell as fast as you can, even if it's a correct mistake." @p.31

- **name**: Management quality assessment as material but difficult component of investment decision
  > "Management always has a big influence on your success, no matter how good or how bad the business is itself. Management is always part of the equation of making the company successful, so the quality of management always matters. But to assess that quality is not that easy." @p.28

- **name**: BYD founding capital-to-scale ratio — $300,000 initial loan scaling to $8 billion revenue and 170,000 employees, no additional capital raised until IPO
  > "This guy, who is a really terrific engineer, started the business from just a $300,000 loan with no additional money until the IPO. He created a company with $8 billion in revenue and 170,000 employees and tens of thousands of engineers." @p.27

- **name**: BYD unit-sales scale-up timeline — from first car produced 8 years prior to over half a million cars/year within under 10 years of market entry, achieved in a fully open competitive market
  > "in less than 10 years, it's selling more than half a million cars a year and has carved out a position for itself." @p.28

- **name**: Long-tenure managerial compensation-to-value-creation ratio (Buffett/Munger) — $100,000 annual salary sustained over 40+ years against hundreds of billions of dollars of created shareholder wealth, cited as the legitimacy anchor for "earned success"
  > "When you create the hundreds of billions in wealth for everybody" @p.29-30

- **name**: Fund closure duration as a scarcity/opportunity signal — nine years closed to new capital, reopened only when investable ideas exceed available capital
  > "The fund has been closed to new investors for nine years. However, we will open it up a bit this year. We have more opportunities than we have money around, but that" @p.29

## Other

- **The Tiananmen-to-Buffett-lecture origin story, told through the "buffet" pun**: Li Lu recounts that he only attended Buffett's Columbia guest lecture because a friend mentioned "a fellow" whose name sounded like "buffet," and Li Lu — desperate and hoping for a free meal — went expecting food, not a talk. This near-comic misunderstanding is presented as the literal accident that redirected his entire life from penniless Tiananmen refugee to value investor, framing his relationship to the discipline as fated rather than planned.

## Concepts

- **title**: Civilization 3.0 compounding loop (S&T × free market) — question: Is this business's growth powered by the self-reinforcing loop of accumulating technology/knowledge plus free-market competition that Li Lu calls "Civilization 3.0" — a loop with no structural ceiling — or is it still bounded by an old-economy hard ceiling on a resource, license, or protected channel?, test: Identify the specific mechanism by which each new unit of technology/knowledge lowers the company's costs or expands its addressable market, and confirm that mechanism is still actively running (not a one-time historical step-change already fully captured)., evidence: Li Lu's account of how modern S&T plus free markets produced open-ended compounding growth unprecedented in agrarian history
  > "A pattern of compounding economic growth over a long span of time began to develop that seemed to have no ends." @line 2207

- **title**: The sixth cardinal relationship — honesty toward strangers as a trust multiplier — question: Does the company extend to strangers (public shareholders, anonymous customers, arms-length counterparties) the same honesty that Chinese relational culture traditionally reserves only for the five cardinal relationships (ruler-subject, father-son, husband-wife, elder-junior, friends)?, test: Look specifically at conduct toward parties with no personal or reciprocal tie to management, since Li Lu argues this is precisely the relationship traditional culture leaves unregulated and where a multiplier effect — of trust built, or of loss from its absence — shows up fastest., evidence: Li Lu's proposal that honesty-toward-strangers become a "6th cardinal relationship" woven into the traditional five, with an explicit multiplier effect on both reward and penalty
  > "Hence, Chinese culture must propose a 6th cardinal relationship to define and govern relationships between strangers." @line 2698

- **title**: Creative destruction vs. the bureaucratic leviathan — question: Could a government administrator plausibly have authorized both this company's rise and its rival's fall — or only an unplanned competitive market could have produced this outcome?, test: Name the incumbent that would have had to be "allowed to fail" for the current leader to win; if no planner could plausibly have sanctioned both the failure and the win, weight the outcome as durable market-driven creative destruction rather than policy-directed allocation a future policy shift could reverse., evidence: Li Lu's Eastman Kodak / Xerox / AT&T-and-Apple examples of creative destruction no government would have chosen
  > "It would be hard to imagine that a government would have chosen to completely destroy AT&T while making Apple, a computer company on the verge of bankruptcy, the winner with the largest market capitalization in the world." @line 2460

- **title**: Equality of opportunity as the primary institutional filter — question: does the institution/company create genuine equality of opportunity (open competition, transparent rules) even though it cannot and should not promise equality of outcome, and does its legitimacy rest on that distinction being honestly maintained?, test: check whether the system's stated openness matches revealed practice — do outsiders without prior connections actually win positions/contracts/promotions at a rate consistent with the stated open process?, evidence: Li Lu's argument that "any human institution that creates equality of opportunity is by definition a hugely beneficial one," contrasted against agrarian-era systems (bloodline inheritance) that could not deliver even that.

## Metrics

- **name**: Value-creation multiplier — 1+1>2 (goods/labor) vs. 1+1>4 (ideas) — formula as stated: Ricardian division-of-labor exchange of goods/services ≈ 1+1>2; free-market exchange of ideas under modern S&T ≈ 1+1>4, because each party keeps its own idea, gains the other's, and new ideas emerge from the exchange itself, reading: a qualitative multiplier, not a literal computation — Li Lu's shorthand for why knowledge/idea-exchanging businesses (open R&D, platforms, networks) compound value faster than businesses that only trade discrete goods, benchNotes: not a computable financial ratio for the cleansing bench — use only as a qualitative screen for whether a business's economics resemble combinatorial knowledge exchange (higher multiplier) or discrete goods trade (bounded, Ricardian gains only); never substitute for reported figures, anchor: business-model
  > "He used the formula 1+1>2 to illustrate this concept." @line 2178

- **name**: Morris Social Development Index (four-trait composite) — formula as stated: four traits — energy capture per person, social organization (population of the largest settlement), information technology, war-making capacity — each capped at 250 points, summing to 1000 at the year-2000 Western benchmark; earlier or other societies are scored by ratio against that benchmark, reading: a historical composite-comparability index; Li Lu's broader point is that a civilization's — or by extension an economy's — development level should be benchmarked on demonstrated capacity to capture and deploy resources across multiple dimensions, not one flow metric alone, benchNotes: illustrative/historical only, not a bench formula to import into company analysis — cite only for the discipline of building weighted, multi-dimensional comparability indices across very different entities or eras, anchor: business-model
  > "Morris divides the ability of a society to capture and expend energy into four traits: energy capture per person, social organization, information technology, and war-making capacity." @line 224

## Other
- **Li Lu's autobiographical framing of the entire treatise**: In the opening chapter, Li Lu directly states his personal stake in this civilizational history — born in China in the 1960s, a decades-long personal obsession with why China lagged, and an "occupational need" as an investor to forecast China's future — framing the entire ~33,500-word treatise as the record of his own thirty-year private inquiry rather than a detached academic exercise.
  > "I, the author, was born in China in the 1960s and have over twenty years of life experience both in China and the United States. My interest in issues of China's modernization started at a young age and has lasted for more than thirty years. My work in investments over the past twenty-odd years has added an occupational need to predict China's future, and during this time, I have been able to accumulate some knowledge and ideas. This series of "Discussions of Modernization" is a record of my 7  musings over the past thirty some years, and I hope they will inspire" @line 175-184

- **The Qianlong Emperor's 1793 reply to King George III, quoted at length as the emblem of China's missed modernization window**: Li Lu reproduces an unusually long verbatim historical document — Emperor Qianlong's letter dismissing Lord Macartney's trade mission and its gifts of the era's most advanced instruments — treating this specific historical text as the symbolic hinge-point where China's imperial self-sufficiency doctrine foreclosed participation in the nascent global market economy, decades before the Opium War made the consequences unavoidable.
  > "Swaying the wide world, I have but one aim in view, namely, to maintain a perfect governance and to fulfill the duties of the State: strange and costly objects do not interest me." @line 1827

- **Shen Kuo as China's own "Renaissance man," five centuries before the Italian Renaissance**: Li Lu constructs an extended, admiring biography of the Song Dynasty polymath Shen Kuo (1031-1095) — comparing him explicitly to Leonardo da Vinci and Benjamin Franklin — as proof that China possessed individuals of world-historic scientific genius, making the eventual "Great Divergence" a matter of institutional and geographic circumstance rather than any deficit of talent or genius.
  > "He was no less a Renaissance man than Leonardo da Vinci or Benjamin Franklin (of the United States). He lived in China, some 500 years before the Italian Renaissance – he was Shen Kuo (1031–1095) of the Song Dynasty (9601279), and the period he lived in happened to be the Chinese Renaissance Era." @line 1738-1742

- **Zheng He's fleet vs. Columbus's, and the counterfactual of a "Trans-Pacific economic circle"**: Li Lu poses an explicit counterfactual — Zheng He's fleet (240+ vessels, ~27,400 sailors, seven expeditions decades before Columbus's four voyages of 30 ships and ~1,940 crew) was technologically capable of discovering the Americas or founding a Pacific trading system, yet the Ming court banned maritime trade in the very same year, 1492, that Columbus reached the New World — a deliberately pointed historical juxtaposition underscoring that the divergence was a matter of political choice, not capability.
  > "By comparison, Columbus only had a total of 30 vessels and 1,940 crew members during his four voyages, and Zheng He preceded Columbus by 70 years." @line 1784-1786

- **The speculative closing vision — humanity leaving Earth, echoing the original migration out of Africa**: In the treatise's final chapter, Li Lu extends his civilizational framework into open futurism — silicon computing surpassing the human brain, human-machine brain compatibility, and eventually humanity being forced to leave Earth once population growth outpaces planetary carrying capacity — explicitly and deliberately mirroring the book's own opening image of early humans venturing out of Africa some 60,000-70,000 years ago, closing the entire work on the same civilizational metaphor it opened with.
  > "At that time, people will have to leave the earth to seek living space on other planets, just as they left Africa some 60,000 years ago." @line 3361-3362

## Concepts

- **title**: Circle of Competence Boundaries — understanding: the competence circle's defining feature is its boundary, not its center; failure to know the limits is equivalent to not understanding
  > "The most important concept in the circle of competence is its boundary" @line 48

- **title**: Cognitive Bias as Systematic Barrier to Rational Investing — evidence: human cognition evolved for survival/reproduction, not market analysis; innate biases make objectivity exceptionally difficult without deliberate training
  > "our minds are the product of natural selection, and their main function is for us to survive and procreate" @line 47

- **title**: Intellectual Honesty as Prerequisite for Real Understanding — test: ability to articulate the strongest counterargument to one's own thesis better than the best informed opponent
  > "if you want to hold an opinion, then you must be able to refute it better than the smartest person you can find who disagrees with you" @line 48

- **title**: Knowledge Compounding Through Passion-Guided Learning — principle: passionate pursuit of a subject creates faster, more effective knowledge accumulation than disinterested study
  > "When you become really interested in something, you can accumulate knowledge about it faster, more effectively and better than anyone else" @line 52

- **title**: Margin of Safety as Protection Against Forecasting Error — function: a sufficient margin of safety allows profitable investment even when understanding is incomplete, because the price protection compensates for predictive uncertainty
  > "When the future isn't clear, you must choose those opportunities which are especially cheap" @line 55

- **title**: Industry Selection as Gating Function for Value Investing — principle: most companies operate in industries with unpredictable competitive outcomes; focus only on industries whose future structure can be forecasted
  > "most companies are too hard and predictions about them cannot be made" @line 58

- **title**: Market Economy as Potential Releaser — insight: market systems enable unconventional people to succeed in niches suited to their unique traits; no uniform template for successful entrepreneurs exists
  > "market economies have a very special property: they can release a person's true potential" @line 62

- **title**: The salesman's self-persuasion trap — the sell-side mindset is dangerous precisely because effective persuasion of others requires first persuading oneself, making the seller his own first victim of the pitch
  > "When you are able to sell something especially well to other people, it's usually because you've persuaded yourself too." @line 39

- **title**: Excellence itself as a discount — Munger's departure from Graham held that a truly excellent company's ability to continuously surpass expectations functions as its own margin of safety, distinct from a statistically cheap price
  > "He never emphasised that these companies had to be bought at a discount because their excellence was in itself a discount as it would allow them to continuously surpass expectations." @line 76

- **title**: Public speaking as a self-deception amplifier — the act of asserting high certainty aloud in public compounds an investor's own confidence beyond what the underlying evidence supports, making public commentary corrosive to intellectual honesty
  > "when you go out and speak in public as if you had 100% certainty, you fool yourself into thinking you have 200% or even 300% certainty" @line 69

- **title**: Investing as a solitary, non-committee pursuit — group deliberation dilutes individual accountability and degrades judgment quality, so the decision-maker must remain personally responsible rather than diffusing responsibility across a committee
  > "value investing is a solitary pursuit in which you must be responsible for the decisions" @line 70

## Metrics

- **name**: Worst-Case Scenario Forecast Horizon — formula: investor demonstrates mastery by articulating company's downside outcome 10 years forward; accuracy verified by retrospective comparison after 10 years elapse
  > "if they really understand a company they're researching, then they must be able to say what will be the worst case for that company after ten years" @line 46

- **name**: Industry Predictability Threshold — formula: only invest in industries where future competitive position can be forecasted with reasonable confidence; reject unpredictable competitive dynamics
  > "most companies are too hard and predictions about them cannot be made" @line 58

- **name**: Portfolio Concentration Limit — formula: research depth sufficient to own only 5-10 companies deeply; reject opportunities outside circle of competence
  > "you shouldn't ever try to study more than five to ten at a time" @line 59

- **name**: Precision vs. Accuracy Trade-off — formula: rough accuracy preferable to false precision; accept confidence threshold but never claim absolute certainty
  > "it's better to be roughly right than precisely wrong" @line 51

- **name**: Management Temperament as Variable by Investment Horizon — formula: for short horizons (0-5yr): manager quality dominates returns; for long horizons (10+ yr): industry dynamics and competitive structure dominate
  > "The longer your investment horizon, the more important industry dynamics become for protecting your moat." @line 57

- **name**: Value Investor Population Estimate — benchmark: approximately 5% of market participants practice value investing; indicates value investor behavior historically sufficient for price discovery despite minority status
  > "But whether it was 5% or 7% or 4% or 10%, the proportion was not particularly high." @line 79

- **name**: Net asset value discount screen — formula: NAV = verified marketable/real-estate asset value minus liabilities; buy signal when transaction price trades near half of computed NAV
  > "If we say the NAV is 100, there are companies which transact at about 50." @line 72

- **name**: Buffett/Munger head-office staffing ratio — formula: >100 subsidiaries and >500,000 employees managed via a head office of only ~25 people, illustrating extreme delegation as an organizational design metric
  > "it has more than 100 subsidiaries and employs more than half a million people but his head office only has about 25 people" @line 67

- **name**: Long-horizon compound-interest multiplier — formula: 7% annual compounding sustained over 200 years compounds to roughly 750,000x, used illustratively to argue for patience over short-term comparison
  > "Compound interest is a gradual force: 7% compounding over 200 years will give you a return of 750,000 times, right?" @line 86

- **name**: China's post-reform growth base rate — formula: ~9% average annual growth sustained across the ~40 years since Reform and Opening, cited as the base-rate case for the power of sustained compounding at the national level
  > "Growth during this time has averaged about 9%, which doesn't sound too high. But in forty short years…" @line 87

  > "If I had never seen Buffett in the flesh, my understanding of stocks would have been stuck on the impression I gained from [Chinese author] Cao Yu's play, "Sunrise"." @line 68

- **Explicit self-description of why he almost never speaks publicly**: Pressed on why he so rarely gives lectures, Li Lu offers an unusually introspective, self-critical answer rooted in his own theory of human psychology — that public speaking amplifies the speaker's own self-deception, compounding stated certainty into false 200-300% confidence — and states this is "personal bias," making an explicit connection between his investment discipline (intellectual honesty, avoiding self-persuasion) and his personal reticence about public appearances, with Columbia and this one Peking University talk cited as the sole exceptions.
  > "It's important to maintain a healthy scepticism because no one can ever be 100% certain." @line 69

- **The philanthropic client-selection policy as a distinctly stated value stance**: Li Lu states that Himalaya Capital deliberately restricts its client base to university endowments, charitable funds, and charity-focused family offices, explicitly framing this as a refusal to "manage money simply to make the rich richer" — a specific, checkable business-model choice presented as a moral position rather than a marketing one.
  > "We only offer our services to university endowments funds, charity funds and family offices focused on charity. We are very picky with our clients and do not manage money simply to make the rich richer." @line 85

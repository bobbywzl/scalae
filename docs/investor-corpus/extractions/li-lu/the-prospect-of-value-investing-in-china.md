## Concepts

- **title**: Stock as Fractional Ownership — question: Are we underwriting the business's own compounding, or trading paper detached from it? test: A share is ownership before it is a tradable instrument; wealth should track intrinsic value compounding with the economy, not the daily tape.
  > "Stock is not only a tradable security but also a certificate representing fractional ownership in the company indeed." @line 457-458

- **title**: Mr. Market as Servant, Not Teacher — question: Is a price move being read as new information about value, or is the market mistaken for the authority on value? test: The market exists to transact with, not instruct — it quotes a price for a stake at any moment but never certifies what that stake is worth.
  > "For value investors, the market exists to serve them and offers an opportunity to acquire ownership." @line 468-469

- **title**: Real Risk Is Permanent Capital Loss, Not Volatility — question: Is "risk" here a real threat to capital, or price movement inside a business still understood and still cheap to intrinsic value? test: Risk turns real once a position sits outside the circle of competence; inside it, swings are noise a margin of safety absorbs.
  > "Only in this sense (investing outside of circle of competence) is there real risk in the market, which is not ups and downs in stock prices, but rather permanent loss of capital." @line 512-513

- **title**: Civilization 3.0 — Free Market Plus Technology as the Growth Engine — question: Is the jurisdiction this business depends on still inside the free-market-plus-technology system that drives sustained compound GDP growth, or has it exited that system? test: Sustained, multi-decade compound GDP growth is a modern (~300-year-old) phenomenon unique to economies combining free exchange with technology; a market's compounding case stands or falls with its place in that system.
  > "I believe China is at interim stage between Civilization 2.0 and Civilization 3.0." @line 1008

- **title**: The "iron law" — the largest market eventually becomes the only market — question: is a company/country positioned inside the largest, most integrated market system, or is it choosing (or being forced into) a smaller, isolated one? test: per Li Lu's reading of Adam Smith/David Ricardo extended to Civilization 3.0, competing market systems converge toward the largest because 1+1>2 (trade synergy) and 1+1>4 (knowledge synergy) compound faster the more participants join; smaller isolated markets structurally fall behind. evidence: East/West Germany, North/South Korea, China-vs-Hong-Kong/Taiwan divergences, and Iran's abandonment of its nuclear program to rejoin the global market are all cited as instances.
  > "In other words, the biggest market will eventually become the only market." @line 932-934

- **title**: Fiduciary duty as innate ("DNA"), not trainable — question: does this manager show behavioral evidence of treating client capital as sacrosanct (a trait Li Lu argues cannot be taught), or only a professed commitment to it? test: ask whether the manager would personally self-select out of the industry upon recognizing the trait's absence — Li Lu explicitly advises those without it not to enter the profession, since no amount of effort substitutes for it. evidence: linked directly to the 2008-09 financial crisis, framed as the aggregate result of widespread fiduciary-duty breaches.
  > "I tend to think that fiduciary duty is something innate, part of someone's DNA." @line 98

## Other
- **Personal origin: attending Warren Buffett's first Columbia lecture "by happenstance"**: Li Lu recounts that ~20 years before this 2015 lecture, he was present, almost by chance, at Warren Buffett's very first investment lecture at Columbia University — a small audience much like the one he's addressing — and this personal proximity to the founding moment of Buffett's teaching legacy anchors his authority to speak on value investing to Chinese students today.
  > "I recall 20 some years ago when I sat in a lecture by Warren Buffett on investment. The audience was as small as today's. It was Warren's first lecture at Columbia University." @line 447-449

- **The "wide open path with no traffic" metaphor for value investing's paradox**: Li Lu develops an extended, distinctive metaphor across the lecture — the true path of value investing is a "wide open" road nobody travels because it looks too long and slow, while the "heterodox paths" of speculative shortcuts are jammed with traffic — explicitly framing career-length patience itself as the scarce resource, not skill or intelligence.
  > "On this wide open main path of investing you find very little traffic and wonder where everybody is. You need only look at the 'heterodox paths' of shortcuts with traffic backed up for miles." @line 561-562

- **China's stakes reframed as civilizational, not merely economic — the "iron law" applied to Iran, the two Koreas, the two Germanys**: Li Lu extends his "iron law of modernization" (the largest market eventually becomes the only market) into a sweeping explanatory device for 20th-century geopolitical divergence — East vs. West Germany, North vs. South Korea, China vs. Hong Kong/Taiwan, and even Iran's decision to abandon its nuclear program — presenting all of these as instances of the same underlying economic law rather than separate political histories.
  > "We can easily explain the disparity between East and West Germany, South and North Korea, China (before reform and opening up) and HK, as well as Taiwan. It helps explain why Iran would abandon its nuclear program, a lifeline, in order to be part of the global market." @line 977-979

- **A blunt personal challenge to the audience: "please heed my advice and don't join this profession"**: Rather than a generic call to ethics, Li Lu tells his student audience directly and repeatedly that anyone who lacks the innate "DNA" for fiduciary duty should not enter asset management at all, since doing so "will cause great harm to society" — an unusually stark gatekeeping statement for a guest lecture aimed at recruiting young talent into the field.
  > "If the answer is no, please heed my advice and don't join this profession. If you do, you will cause great harm to society." @line 659-660

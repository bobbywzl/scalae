## Concepts

- **title**: Downside protection as the portfolio's anchor — losses are limited by asset coverage; upside emerges from patience and eventual price discovery toward intrinsic value
  > "when you got them too cheap, they maybe ended up going down the tubes. So you try to be a little careful. But people don't like to buy things that are going down." @line 199-204

- **title**: Contrarian discipline under crowding pressure — as value investing becomes fashionable, the margin of safety erodes and the advantage diminishes
  > "all the publicity about value investing—it's become a very popular thing. A lot of people are doing it now, and not just talking about it. So it gets tougher and tougher." @line 430-434

- **title**: Refusal to hold or act on a macro/market-direction opinion — Graham's working-capital-stock scarcity heuristic as the only permitted "market call," and even that is treated skeptically once it stopped working
  > "have no opinion on the market. Graham used to have this theory that if there were no working capital stocks around, that meant the market was too high," @line 1359-1364

- **title**: Passive, non-activist ownership stance — buy the security, not the business; never seek control or push management
  > "I am a passive investor. There are people who are very aggressive; they try to buy companies." @line 397-398

- **title**: Preference for being acquired over acquiring — capital allocation discipline that avoids the systematic overpayment risk buyers face
  > "that's why I'm really not much of a buyer of companies: | like to be the guy on the other end, being taken over." @line 1239-1243

- **title**: Circle-of-competence discipline extended to whole industries, not just individual securities — high-tech and "go-go" sectors excluded regardless of apparent statistical cheapness
  > "I don't want to be in the com-puler industry, or things like that, where I don't really under-stand those companies." @line 1076-1079

- **title**: Full-investment policy as a psychological, not analytical, choice — Schloss never holds meaningful cash even absent an active opinion on market level
  > "We've never really done that. We've been always fully invested." @line 1269-1271

- **title**: Removed, non-emotional posture toward daily price action — deliberately avoiding a ticker-tape machine and the fear/greed cycle it feeds
  > "And valuc-oriented. 1 try to be removed from the day-to-day; I don't have a ticker-tape machine in my office." @line 411-413

## Metrics

- **name**: Working capital per share (classic) / Book value per share (modern) — formula as practiced: current assets minus current liabilities per share; adjusted for long-term debt; buy at steep discount (two-thirds or deeper) to ensure margin of safety
  > "We basically followed the idea of buying companies selling below working capital—at two-thirds of working capital—then, when the stocks' prices went up to match working capital per share, we'd have made 50% on our money." @line 170-175

- **name**: Asset-coverage multiples on liquidation basis — valuation check to ensure intrinsic value floor even if operations fail; management ownership alignment as secondary indicator
  > "you couldn't replace it for what it sells for." @line 997-999

- **name**: Historical firm-level compound return on the working-capital strategy — formula: ~20% per year while eligible working-capital-discount stocks remained available, reading: return attributable specifically to the classic Graham net-net method before those opportunities dried up in the 1950s
  > "The firm averaged about 20% a year on that basis, and it was a great deal, as long as these stocks were around." @line 176-178

- **name**: Average holding period — formula: approximately four years per position, reading: benchmark used to judge whether continued holding of an appreciated stock is still supported by the original thesis
  > "would say our average holding period is four years—and things have been developing nicely, it may change your benchmark." @line 296-299

- **name**: Price-to-earnings and yield check on high-quality consumer names — formula as stated: book ~$27/share, EPS ~$4, price $40 (10x earnings), dividend $2.20 — reading: even non-net-net "good value" holdings are sized against a replacement-cost/earnings sanity check
  > "It has a book of maybe 27, and it's got earnings of $4 a share, I'd say. It sells at 40; the dividend is $2.20, so it's selling at 10 times earnings." @line 815-819

## Other

- **The GEICO origin story**: Schloss was present in Graham's office the day Graham first bought Government Employees Insurance Co. (GEICO) — Graham had "no conception of the growth potential," framing the buy purely as downside-protected cheapness rather than a growth bet, even though the position became one of the most celebrated investments in value-investing history via Buffett.
  > "Walter, if this doesn't work out, we can always liquidate it and get our money back," @line 234-236

- **Modest, self-funded founding of the partnership**: Schloss's own firm began in 1955 with just 19 partners putting up $5,000 apiece (one at $10,000) — a total of $100,000 — a deliberately unglamorous origin he calls "kind of pathetic" next to modern funds that won't start without $10 million.
  > "got myself 19 partners, and they each put up $5,000. One guy put up $10,000." @line 366-367

- **Ben Graham returning investors' money rather than chase scale**: Schloss recalls Graham managing only $5–10 million and writing partners around 1953 to say they had more capital than the strategy could use — an aphoristic contrast Schloss draws against his own later discomfort with his fund's growth to $45 million.
  > "Ben Graham had $5 million in Graham-Newman Corp. he started up a partnership which had $10 million called Newman & Graham; and he wrote the partners, somewhere around '53, "We really can't use all this money, please take some of it back."" @line 578-586

- **Preference to serve ordinary people, not the already-wealthy**: A distinctly plain-spoken statement of who Schloss chose to manage money for — not sophisticated wealthy clients who "don't need me," but ordinary partners who "need some help," reflecting his low-key, service-oriented self-conception.
  > "I really didn't want to find a lot of enormously wealthy people and manage their money." @line 386-388

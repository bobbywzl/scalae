## Other

- **The recurring "shut up, you idiot" mental-hospital anecdote**: Schloss opens this Harvard lecture with the same self-deprecating story he used elsewhere (a psychiatric patient telling him to "shut up" during an investment talk, which the therapist called "the first intelligent thing" said in months) — a signature piece of his plain-spoken, humble public persona reused across multiple talks.
  > "Shut up, you idiot and sit down". I turned to the therapist and asked him what I should do." @line 19-20

- **"Making Money Out of Junk" — the 1973 Forbes profile title**: Schloss references the self-effacing headline Forbes gave his own 1973 profile, embracing rather than resisting the unglamorous "junk"-buying image of his statistical, out-of-favor-stock strategy.
  > "back in 1973, Forbes Magazine wrote an article about me modestly entitled, "Making Money Out of Junk"." @line 24-26

- **The $640 Saks dress anecdote**: A distinctly personal, symbolic story — Schloss's daughter wanted to buy his Depression-era wife a $640 dress at Saks; the wife was "horrified at the price and brought it back," illustrating in miniature the family's inherited thrift and aversion to overpaying that also governs Schloss's stock selection.
  > "My daughter thought it would be nice to go to Saks Fifth Avenue in New York and buy my wife a dress for $640, plus tax that wasn't on sale." @line 47-49

- **"If the other fellow is laid off it's a Recession, but if you are laid off it's a Depression"**: A distinctly plain-spoken personal aphorism Schloss uses to explain how his own family's Depression-era experience — not abstract theory — permanently shaped his downside-protection-first investment posture.
  > "if the other fellow is laid off it's a Recession, but if you are laid off it's a Depression." @line 85-86

- **The Coca-Cola shopkeeper story from Jamaica**: A personal travel anecdote — a chance conversation with a woman whose grandmother sold a Depression-era coffee shop and invested the proceeds in Coca-Cola stock, which by the time of the lecture was reportedly worth millions — used by Schloss to illustrate long-term compounding, told in his own understated voice.
  > "her grandmother had a little coffee shop there during the Depression. She sold the shop and took the money and invested it in Coke." @line 134-135

- **Gratitude toward Tweedy, Browne's Howard Browne**: Schloss closes the lecture by publicly thanking Howard Browne (Christopher Browne's father) for giving him a desk in the Tweedy, Browne office decades earlier — a personal-history detail tying Schloss's entire independent career to that one act of generosity.
  > "When Howard Browne, Chris Browne's father at Tweedy, Browne let me have a desk in his office, he never thought I'd still be there 40 years later," @line 165-167

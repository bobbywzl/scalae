## Concepts

- **title**: Margin of Safety — the protective buffer between purchase price and calculated intrinsic value, reducing both downside risk and the need for precise valuation accuracy.
  > "That is what Ben Graham meant by having a margin of safety. You don't try and buy businesses worth $83 million for $80 million. You leave yourself an enor-mous margin." @line 932-935

- **title**: Price versus Value Discrepancy — the central insight that market prices reflect emotion and herd behavior rather than rational assessment of underlying business worth, creating exploitable gaps.
  > "These Graham-and-Doddsville investors have successfully exploited gaps between price and value." @line 833-834

- **title**: Intellectual Origin as Causal Factor — the observation that concentrated success among a specific educational lineage (Graham's students) suggests teachable principles rather than luck, analogous to epidemiological clustering.
  > "if you found any really extraordinary concentrations of success, you might want to see if you could identify concentrations of un-usual characteristics that might be causal factors." @line 150-153

- **title**: Inverted risk/reward relationship at deeper discounts — the cheaper the price relative to value, the lower the true risk, contradicting beta-based risk models
  > "if you buy a dollar bill for 60 cents, it's riskier than if you buy a dollar bill for 40 cents, but the expectation of reward is greater in the latter case." @line 894-897

- **title**: Style diversity within a shared discipline — concentrated vs. widely diversified portfolios, small-cap vs. large-cap, can all express the same underlying price/value discipline with near-zero name overlap
  > "throughout this whole period there was prac-tically no duplication in these portfolios." @line 564-566

- **title**: Independence from consensus/influence as a durable trait of the successful disciple — Schloss cited by name as uniquely resistant to Buffett's own influence over stock selection
  > "That's one of his strengths: no one has much influence on him." @line 479-480

## Other

- **Buffett's own origin story of trying to work for Graham for free**: A distinctly personal, symbolic anecdote — Buffett offered to work at Graham-Newman for nothing after taking Graham's class, and Graham turned him down as "overvalued," a line Buffett uses to show how seriously Graham applied his own valuation discipline even to hiring decisions.
  > "I offered to go to work at Graham-Newman for nothing after I took Ben Graham's class, but he turned me down as overvalued." @line 394-396

- **Buffett's direct tribute to Walter Schloss's character and method**: Buffett singles Schloss out by name as the first "superinvestor" case study, describing him as an outsider with no Wall Street connections who succeeds purely by reading numbers in manuals and annual reports — praise built on Schloss's total lack of pretense or inside access.
  > "He has no connections or access to useful informa-tion. Practically no one in Wall Street knows him and he is not fed any ideas." @line 413-415

- **Schloss's total integrity and aversion to loss, in Buffett's ("Adam Smith's") words**: A quoted secondhand tribute (from Adam Smith's Supermoney, relayed by Buffett) describing Schloss's psychological makeup — his strong aversion to loss stemming from handling other people's money, and his unusually realistic, unpretentious self-image.
  > "He never forgets that he is handling other people's money and this reinforces his normal strong aversion to loss." @line 461-462

- **Schloss's imperviousness to even Buffett's own influence**: Despite being close friends and having worked together at Graham-Newman, Buffett notes he has essentially no sway over Schloss's stock selections — an anecdote framed as one of Schloss's defining professional strengths.
  > "That's one of his strengths: no one has much influence on him." @line 479-480

- **The list of deliberately obscure Schloss holdings**: Buffett names Schloss's largest positions — Hudson Pulp & Paper, Jeddo Highland Coal, New York Trap Rock Company — as illustrations of Schloss's willingness to own completely unglamorous, name-unrecognized companies purely on statistical cheapness.
  > "Walter's largest holdings have been such stalwarts as Hudson Pulp & Paper and Jeddo Highland Coal and New York Trap Rock Company" @line 569-571

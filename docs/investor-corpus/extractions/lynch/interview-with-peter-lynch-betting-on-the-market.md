## Concepts

- **title**: "Few good stocks in a lifetime" — the thesis that portfolio success requires concentration on a handful of big winners, not perfection or activity
  > "You only need a few good stocks in your lifetime. I mean how many times do you need a stock to go up ten-fold to make a lot of money? Not a lot." @line 114

- **title**: "Temperament beats timing" — portfolio returns depend more on investor stomach for volatility and holding power than on predicting market turning points
  > "In this business if you're good, you're right six times out of ten. You're never going to be right nine times out of ten." @line 135

- **title**: "Homework vs. speculation" — the gap between informed analysis (understanding business, product, customer behavior) and uninformed gambling
  > "When they buy a refrigerator, they do research. When they buy a microwave oven, they do research. They'll get Consumer Reports . They'll ask a customer "What's your favorite kind of oven?" @line 649

- **title**: "Rock-turning discipline" — investment outperformance correlates directly with effort and curiosity; winner is whoever examines most investment opportunities
  > "The person that turns over the most rocks wins the game. And that's always been my philosophy." @line 206

- **title**: "Watering the weeds and cutting the flowers" — the folly of selling winning positions early while holding onto losing ones, the inverse of the discipline that actually compounds wealth
  > "Some stocks go up 20-30 percent and they get rid of it and they hold onto the dogs. And it's sort of like watering the weeds and cutting out the flowers." @line 123-124

- **title**: "Stomach over brain" — the deciding factor in surviving market corrections and bear markets is psychological/emotional tolerance for volatility, not intellectual analysis
  > "stomach is the key organ here. It's not the brain. Do you have the stomach for these kind of declines?" @line 585-586

- **title**: "Fear vs. greed as a 5,000-year-old constant" — the behavioral cycle that drives investors to sell into declines and buy into rallies is an ancient, unchanging feature of human nature, not a modern market anomaly
  > "human nature hasn't changed much in 5,000 years. There's this thing of greed versus fear." @line 703-704

- **title**: "Direct calls over consensus forecasting" — actionable recessionary signal comes from calling companies directly to check on their own order flow and inventory, not from macro forecasts or economist predictions
  > "That's crystal ball stuff. That doesn't work. Futile." @line 506-507

## Metrics

- **name**: Historical annual return to stocks over long horizons — formula: ~11% total return (capital appreciation + dividends), divided into ~8% corporate profit growth + ~2–3% dividend yield; historical corporate profit growth ~8% annually (doubling every 9 years, quadrupling every 18 years, sixfold every 25 years)
  > "Historically, corporate profits have grown about eight percent a year. Eight percent a year. They double every nine years. They quadruple every 18. They go up six-fold every 25 years." @line 608

- **name**: Market volatility frequency — formula: 53 corrections of 10%+ in 95 years = approximately once every two years; 15 bear markets of 25%+ = approximately once every six years
  > "The market itself is very volatile. We've had 95 years completed this century. We're in the middle of 1996 and we're close to a 10 percent decline. In the 95 years so far, we've had 53 declines in the market of 10 percent or more." @line 569

- **name**: Timing impact on returns — benchmark: investing at market high every year for 30 years yields 10.6% return; investing at market low every year yields 11.7% return; difference across 30 years is marginal compared to holding discipline
  > "In the 30 years, 1965 to 1995, if you had invested a thousand dollars, you had incredible good luck, you invested a the low of the year, you picked the low day of the year, you put your thousand dollars in, your return would have been 11.7 compounded." @line 679

- **name**: Magellan Fund asset growth trajectory — formula as stated: $20 million when Lynch took over in May 1977, passed $1 billion in April 1983, $14 billion when he left in 1990, over $50 billion by the time of the interview (1996)

## Other

- **Origin story rooted in caddying, not finance training**: Lynch traces his very first exposure to the stock market to overhearing corporate executives discuss stocks while caddying as an 11-year-old, a formative biographical anecdote that grounds his populist "anyone can do this" framing.
  > "I started caddying when I was 11. So the would have been 1955 and in that part -- the '50s were a great decade for the stock market." @line 72-73

- **Explicit "you have to know the story" as invest-in-what-you-know framing**: Lynch directly links doing homework on a handful of familiar companies (versus blind speculation) to the ability to act with real edge, an early, direct articulation of the philosophy that became his signature.
  > "You have to know the story. And people have lots of edges and they throw them away." @line 564

- **"Ten bagger" baseball-analogy origin story**: Lynch personally explains the etymology of his own famous coined term, tracing it to his lifelong love of baseball and the long championship drought of the Boston Red Sox/Braves-adjacent culture — a distinctive folksy touch that shows how his vocabulary was built from everyday life, not finance jargon.
  > "I've always been a great lover of baseball. I mean if you grew up in Boston, you know that the last time we won the World Series, Babe Ruth pitched for us." @line 104-106

- **The wife-in-the-supermarket L'eggs story as the purest "invest in what you know" anecdote**: Carolyn Lynch's enthusiasm for a hosiery product discovered in an ordinary shopping trip becomes one of Lynch's biggest and most famous positions — the canonical real-world illustration of sourcing ideas from everyday consumer life rather than Wall Street research.
  > "Tell the story about your wife stumbling on a big stock for you in the supermarket." @line 433-434

- **1990 crash commentary naming Saddam Hussein's invasion and the banking crisis as the true source of fear**: Lynch's own retrospective ranking of 1990 (not 1987) as the scariest period of his 30-year career, tied to concrete geopolitical and systemic-banking triggers rather than abstract market anxiety.
  > "Ninety, in my 30 years of watching stock very carefully, was by far the scariest period." @line 381-382

- **Magellan Fund track-record color: the "$1,000 to $28,000" statement in his own words**: Lynch's direct, first-person restatement of Magellan's growth under his management, the number he is most associated with across public appearances.
  > "if somebody invested a thousand dollars in Magellan on May 31st, 1977, the day I left, the thousand would have been $28,000, --May 31st 13 years later, 1990." @line 295-297

- **Self-deprecating humor about accountability for fund performance**: a distinctive folksy aside about what would have happened to his public image had Magellan underperformed, underscoring his plain-spoken humility about the role luck and results play in reputation.
  > "If I'd had gone down, I'd have had to dye my hair and grow a beard and move to Fiji." @line 285-286

## Concepts
- **title**: Rational vs. Irrational Biotech Valuation — The industry values companies on "hopes and dreams" rather than earnings, creating systematic overvaluation of unproven candidates
  > "between the end of 1991 and February 1993, the market value of the entire industry declined from about $41 billion to $26 billion, which reminds us how tenuous these valuations can be, based as they are on hopes and dreams, as opposed to earnings." @line 101-104
- **title**: Prediction Humility in Biotech Selection — Even insider scientists and analysts fail to predict which drug candidates will succeed; insider advantage is negated by inherent uncertainty
  > "Apparently, the experts are as much in the dark about how to pick the next Genentech from among the many potential Genexes as the average person with a degree in English lit." @line 123-125
- **title**: Burn Rate as Survival Metric — Companies' ability to sustain operations depends on cash reserves and spending rate, not on the promise of their research
  > "Some concentrate on the cash, and pick companies with enough of a bankroll to survive several years at their current level of spending, which is called the "burn rate."" @line 156-158
- **title**: Objective vs. Subjective Trial Endpoints — a drug candidate whose efficacy can be proven with a simple, quantifiable measurement carries less regulatory risk than one requiring a harder-to-prove subjective outcome
  > "Neupogen did one simple thing that was easy to measure: It raised the white blood-cell count." @line 147-148
- **title**: Sector Contagion Risk — because biotech valuations rest on hopes rather than earnings, one company's Phase III failure triggers sympathetic sell-offs across unrelated biotech stocks with no shared drug or trial
  > "Moreover, when one biotech company fails a big test, the others are sold off in sympathy, as they were after Synergen's drug flopped last February." @line 98-100
- **title**: Investor's Edge Inapplicability in Science-Driven Industries — Lynch's signature consumer/on-the-job edge concept explicitly breaks down in a field where outcomes hinge on unobservable lab science and regulatory review rather than visible business trends
  > "the investor's edge I'm always talking about often doesn't work in biotech." @line 168-169

## Concepts
- **title**: Windfall from mutual to public ownership structure — The unique capital dynamics of converting a mutually-owned depositor-owned bank into a shareholder-owned public company
  > "This mass ownership is what creates a windfall for the new shareholders, as I'll explain in more detail below." @line 69-70

- **title**: Cash-in-till mechanism — Public offering capital flows entirely to company balance sheet (no prior owners to compensate), instantly doubling book value at conversion
  > "The proceeds from a public offering are counted as equity, which is why converted savings banks excel in this category." @line 201-202

- **title**: First-mover consolidation advantage — Mutual savings banks with strong conversion economics become acquisition targets for larger institutions at premiums to book value
  > "By acquiring smaller banks and thrifts, they can expand their deposit bases and eventually increase their earnings." @line 336-337

- **title**: Retail-democratic IPO access as a structural edge for the individual investor — unlike ordinary IPOs where institutions grab the allocation, mutual-to-stock conversions let ordinary depositors buy at the same initial price insiders and professionals do — test: Does the offering vest based on account/deposit history rather than broker relationships or institutional allocation?
  > "conversions are the rare exception where the little guy gets to buy shares at the initial offering price" @line 73-74

- **title**: Insider buying at the identical price as a confidence signal — because corporate insiders would not risk their own capital unless they expect a gain, an investor paying the same conversion price as officers/directors is implicitly following informed money — test: Are officers/directors buying meaningful share allocations at the same offering price disclosed in the prospectus?
  > "Corporate insiders are not known to be self-destructive. They don't buy company stock unless they expect to make money on it. So when you pay the same price they do, you're in with the right crowd." @line 245-248

## Metrics
- **name**: Equity-to-assets ratio (e/a) — Formula: equity divided by assets; benchmark 5% considered good, converted thrifts average 9%, Green Point Savings post-conversion achieved 20%
  > "The equity-to-assets ratio is the standard measure of financial strength, and an e/a of 5 percent is considered good." @line 202-203

- **name**: Nonperforming assets percentage — Formula: bad loans and delinquent accounts divided by total assets; target less than 0.5 percent as comforting threshold
  > "I look for thrifts that have a low percentage of nonperformers. Less than 0.5 percent is comforting." @line 222-223

- **name**: Return on assets (ROA) — Formula: annual bank net income divided by average total assets; benchmark 1 percent average, above 1 percent is positive signal
  > "The common measure of a bank's profitability is return on assets. You get the ROA by dividing the bank's income by its assets over a specified period. A 1 percent ROA is average, and that's a good benchmark to use." @line 225-228

- **name**: Price-to-earnings ratio (p/e) — Formula: stock price per share divided by earnings per share; Lynch targets thrifts with p/e ratios below 10
  > "Another useful measure is the price-to-earnings ratio. I look for thrifts with p/e ratios below 10." @line 229-230

- **name**: Book value per share — Derived from bank's equity divided by outstanding shares post-IPO; historically 384 traded thrifts sell at 104 percent of book value in mature positions
  > "On average, the 384 thrifts that trade on the major exchanges sell for 104 percent of book value in today's market." @line 169-170

- **name**: Merger/acquisition premium to book — formula: average acquisition price as a multiple of book value across a peer study, reading: 1.5x book was the average paid for the 16 thrifts acquired in 1993, and every deal cleared 300% of the original IPO offering price — used to estimate takeout value of a still-public thrift
  > "The average acquisition price was 1.5 times book. Not a single one was acquired at a price less than 300 percent of the initial offering price for the shares." @line 360-363

- **name**: Sector consolidation rate — formula: % of a peer universe (thrifts/savings banks) acquired via merger/buyout over a trailing period; reading: 37% over five years signals structural M&A tailwind supporting valuations even in a fragmented industry
  > "In the past five years, nearly 37 percent of the thrifts and savings banks have been acquired through mergers and buyouts." @line 320-321

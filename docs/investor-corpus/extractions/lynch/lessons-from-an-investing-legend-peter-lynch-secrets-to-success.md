## Concepts

- **title**: Research rigor vs. gambling — How to distinguish genuine analysis from speculation
  > "They'll put $5,000 or $10,000 on some zany idea they heard on the bus. That's gambling. That's not investing. That's not research. That's just total speculation." @line 310

- **title**: Edge availability — Personal knowledge that informs fundamental understanding
  > "Ask yourself: Do you know something about the company? What can you add to the math? Do you have an edge?" @line 329

- **title**: Professional-domain edge (the cardiologist example) — question/test: Can an investor's occupational vantage point let them see a company's breakthrough before the market does? Evidence: an interventional cardiologist witnessing a heart-pump procedure firsthand, "way ahead of most people." Test: Am I professionally positioned to see this company's product/service working (or failing) before Wall Street notices?
  > "You could be an interventional cardiologist and you put in a heart pump. You say, wow, this really is an incredible breakthrough, preventing shock, providing hemodynamic support." @line 331

- **title**: Paper-portfolio practice discipline — question/test: Can a prospective investor build genuine stock-picking judgment without capital at risk, by tracking a large hypothetical portfolio and interrogating the reason for each pick? Evidence: Lynch's explicit "$10,000 or $100,000" paper-portfolio exercise, picking 5 companies and asking "why am I buying these 5?" Test: Can I articulate a non-momentum reason ("the sucker's going up" is disqualified) for each holding, and would I still hold it after tracking the story over time?
  > "Start with a paper portfolio. Then say: I'm going to put together X amount of dollars in the portfolio—say $10,000 or $100,000. It's paper. Make it big. Pick 5 companies to buy." @line 321

- **title**: Sell discipline tied to maturity, not price — question/test: Is the decision to exit driven by the same story-based logic as the decision to buy, rather than by a price target? Evidence: Taco Bell's southern-California-to-everywhere expansion as the model growth story; a company moving "from crummy to semi-crummy to getting better" argues for holding, while one nearing full penetration or with a deteriorating story argues for selling. Test: Has the original growth story matured or deteriorated, independent of the stock's recent price action?
  > "Deciding when to sell is exactly the same thing as when you buy it. You have a certain story—why I bought this." @line 344

- **title**: Batting-average asymmetry (being wrong 40-50% of the time and still winning) — question/test: Does a portfolio need a high hit rate to succeed, or does asymmetric payoff structure (large multi-baggers vs. capped losses) dominate? Evidence: Lynch's own "so many flops" admission — a fashion retailer with no debt wiped out by a hemline shift — set against "right 5 or 6 times out of 10" combined with winners that go "4- or 10- or 20-fold." Test: Am I evaluating a strategy by win-rate alone, or by the asymmetry between capped downside (100% loss max) and uncapped upside (multi-bagger)?
  > "So you have flops. Maybe you're right 5 or 6 times out of 10. But if your winners go up 4- or 10- or 20-fold, it makes up for the ones where you lost 50%, 75%, or 100%." @line 365

- **title**: Investor bias as the real edge-eroder, not information scarcity — question/test: Has the informational edge amateur investors historically lacked actually closed (telecast earnings, free investor presentations, instant balance-sheet access), such that the remaining edge gap is behavioral/psychological (biases against unglamorous sectors) rather than access to data? Evidence: contrast between waiting for mailed annual reports in Lynch's early career and today's free, worldwide-telecast investor disclosures; the resulting bias toward only "companies growing 40% a year" and against railroads, oil, steel, turnarounds, unionized companies. Test: Am I avoiding a sector/company because of genuine analysis, or because of an unexamined stylistic bias?
  > "So theoretically the individual's edge has improved in the last 20, 30 years versus the professional. The problem is people have so many biases." @line 372

## Metrics

- **name**: Economic forecast predictive value — Minutes spent annually on economic forecasting vs. years of stock market study
  > "I think if you spent over 13 minutes a year on economics, you've wasted over 10 minutes." @line 354

- **name**: Magellan Fund AUM growth under Lynch — formula: assets under management growth over a defined 13-year tenure, stated in absolute dollar terms ($18 million to $14 billion, 1977-1990). Bench notes: a headline scale metric of realized fund performance, not a return percentage, but establishes the magnitude Lynch's stock-picking record is measured against.
  > "For the 13 years that Lynch ran Fidelity's Magellan ® Fund (1977–1990), he earned a reputation as a top performer, increasing assets under management from $18 million to $14 billion (as of 1990)." @line 258

- **name**: Market-penetration expansion runway (Walmart case) — formula: percentage of total addressable geography penetrated at time of initial interest (15%) vs. full penetration (100%) achieved over the following 30 years, correlated with a 50-fold stock return. Bench notes: used as the concrete evidentiary anchor for "buy in the second or third inning" — the penetration percentage itself is the inning-clock.
  > "Walmart was in only 15% of the United States when they were a 10-year-old public company. All they did for the next 30 years was go from 15% to 100%. The stock went up 50-fold." @line 340

- **name**: Hit-rate vs. payoff asymmetry ratio — formula: acceptable win rate of ~50-60% ("right 5 or 6 times out of 10") offset by winners returning 4x-20x against capped losses of 50-100%. Bench notes: an explicit numeric statement of the asymmetric-bet structure underlying concentrated stock-picking; frames portfolio success as payoff-weighted, not accuracy-weighted.
  > "So you have flops. Maybe you're right 5 or 6 times out of 10. But if your winners go up 4- or 10- or 20-fold, it makes up for the ones where you lost 50%, 75%, or 100%." @line 365

- **name**: Historical decline frequency during a fund tenure — formula: count of 10%+ market declines observed across a fixed multi-year span (9 declines of 10%+ across Lynch's 13 years running Magellan), used as a base-rate reference for how often the "stomach test" gets triggered. Bench notes: distinct from the bear-market-musings 95-year base rate; this is a manager's lived, tenure-specific count rather than a market-history statistic.
  > "Over the 13 years I ran Magellan ® the market went down 9 times 10% or more. Every time it went down, the fund went down more." @line 284

## Other

- **Early-life origin of Lynch's investing career**: Lynch traces his path into stocks directly to caddying as a boy after his father's early death — a formative, non-analytical entry point (watching stocks "go up" while listening to golfers) rather than a formal training story, and a rare piece of hard biographical color from the man himself.
  > "My father took sick when I was 7, died when I was 10. I started caddying at 11 in the '50s when the stock market was great." @line 299

- **First stock pick and its stated purpose**: Lynch names his actual first investment — Flying Tiger airlines, bought on a $300 stake funded by a college research report on the air-cargo industry and the Vietnam War buildup — and credits it with paying for graduate school, a concrete origin story behind the "know your edge" doctrine he teaches decades later.
  > "At Boston College, I did a report on the air cargo industry, and I bought $300 worth of Flying Tiger airlines." @line 301

- **Self-deprecating age framing**: at 75, Lynch jokes about his own definition of "middle age" and "old," a distinctive piece of personal humor that frames his decades of continuity in investing style ("I haven't changed at all").
  > "I'm 75 now (I think middle age is 88 and old's 107). I haven't changed at all." @line 305

- **The Vogue-dress flop story**: Lynch's most vivid personal failure anecdote — a debt-free fashion retailer whose entire product line was rendered obsolete overnight by a single Hollywood film (Bonnie and Clyde) shifting hemline fashion — used to humanize the "you will have flops" lesson with a specific, memorable, non-abstract example.
  > "One time I bought a retailer with these great dresses. They were on the cover of Vogue 3 times, and the company had no debt, which is really important." @line 363

- **Physical/scene-setting detail of the interview itself**: the piece notes Lynch's appearance and setting — sneakers with a suit, visiting Fidelity's old hand-drawn Chart Room — grounding the whole Q&A as a real, dated encounter (50th Fidelity anniversary) rather than a compiled quote list.
  > "Looking spry in a dark blue suit, yellow tie, and sneakers, he recently popped into Fidelity's Chart Room" @line 256

- **Post-Magellan philanthropic identity**: beyond investing, the piece notes Lynch's sustained charitable ties to Boston institutions — a biographical detail consistent with his later Worth columns on corporate volunteerism, showing continuity between his public investing persona and his civic life.
  > "has been a generous contributor to the Boston community, the Catholic Schools Foundation and the Inner City Scholarship Fund." @line 258

## Concepts

- **title**: Market Timing Futility — investors attempt to dodge corrections by switching in/out of stocks, but timing discipline destroys returns; no billionaires made their wealth through market timing
  > "Warren Buffett weighs in at No. 2 on the Forbes list. He got there by picking stocks and not by switching in and out of them." @line 175

- **title**: Dividend Growth + Dividend Reinvestment as Correction Buffer — portfolio structured 100% in dividend-paying companies; withdrawal strategy uses rising dividends to reduce stock sales over time; corrections become rebalancing opportunities
  > "You sink 100 percent of your investment capital into a portfolio of companies that pay regular dividends." @line 221

- **title**: Growth Stock Resilience in Corrections — growth companies (software, drugs, food chains) outperform cyclical stocks during downturns; historically 230-fold returns vs 19-fold for cyclicals since 1949
  > "I've long been a fan of growth companies, but in the course of collecting data for this article, I was amazed to discover that since 1949, an investment in the 50 growth stocks on Safian's list has returned over 230-fold, while the cyclicals have returned only 19-fold." @line 211

- **title**: Hedging as portfolio self-sabotage — buying options/futures as "correction insurance" seems cheap at first but compounds cost over time as expiring contracts must be repeatedly renewed, and creates a psychological conflict between rooting for stocks to fall (insurance payoff) versus rise (portfolio gain) — test: does the investor's stated hedge actually reduce net portfolio volatility once renewal costs are amortized, or just add a recurring drag?
  > "the options expire every couple of months, and if stocks don't go down on schedule, people have to buy more options to renew the policy. Suddenly, investing isn't so simple." @line 153

- **title**: "Lightening up" as disguised market timing — investors who partially sell stocks or sit on cash "waiting for the crash" still call themselves long-term investors, but have functionally become timers whose success depends on skill they likely don't have — evidence: cash sitters who wait for further declines that never come and miss the rebound
  > "Or they put off buying stocks in companies they like and sit on their cash, waiting for the crash." @line 157

- **title**: The zero-bonds dividend-harvesting strategy — replacing a bond's income stream with a rising-dividend equity portfolio, selling a shrinking slice of stock each year to supplement the growing dividend, so that eventually the dividend alone covers the withdrawal need while principal keeps compounding — test: does the withdrawal rate stay below the combined dividend growth + price appreciation rate over a 20-year horizon?
  > "Every year there after, as dividends are raised and stock prices go up, you're selling less and less stock to cover your expenses. In year 16, you receive $7,000 out of the dividends alone" @line 239

## Metrics

- **name**: Historical correction frequency — formula: 53 occurrences of 10%+ decline over ~100 years (turn of the century to 1995) = roughly one correction every two years; 15 of those 53 were severe (25%+ decline / bear markets) = one nasty correction every six years

- **name**: Long-term stock return composition — formula: 8% annual corporate profit growth + 3% dividend yield = 11% total annual return; even if profit growth drops to 6%, 5%, or 4%, stock returns still exceed bond returns when dividends included
  > "Historically, corporate profits have advanced by 8 percent a year. This 8 percent, along with the 3 percent dividend yield, is what accounts for the 11 percent annual return." @line 269

- **name**: Cost of market timing via missed-months analysis — S&P 500 1954-1995: missing 10 most profitable months reduces 11.4% return to 8.3%; missing 20 most profitable months drops to 6.1%; missing 40 months yields only 2.7% (underperforms savings account)
  > "If you missed the 20 most profitable months, your return was 6.1 percent; the 40 most profitable, and you made only 2.7 percent." @line 165

- **name**: Average annual stock volatility — most individual stocks fluctuate 50 percent from top to bottom annually without market-wide fanfare; index focus makes corrections visible and psychologically damaging
  > "Most stocks fluctuate 50 percent from top to bottom every year, without any fanfare." @line 135

- **name**: Worst-case-scenario stress test on the dividend-harvesting strategy — formula: even if the portfolio takes an immediate 25% overnight loss the day after full investment and then a 20-year recession halves both price appreciation (to 4%) and dividend growth (to 1.5%), the withdrawal plan still returns principal roughly equal to the bond alternative, and under the milder 25%-shock-only scenario ends 20 years later at $185,350 versus $100,000 for bonds

- **name**: Historical bonds-vs-stocks century count — formula: bonds outperformed stocks in only one decade of the 20th century (the 1930s) and came close only once more (the 1980s); by the first half of the 1990s stocks had again proven superior

- **name**: 1966-1982 "lost decade" total return floor — formula: even during the Dow's stagnant stretch from a peak of 1000 to a nadir of 777, dividend-inclusive S&P 500 total return was 75.37% over 16 years, or 3.5% annualized — evidence that dividends cushion even prolonged flat markets

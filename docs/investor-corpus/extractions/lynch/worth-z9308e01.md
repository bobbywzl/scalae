## Concepts

- **title**: Pan-and-shovel technique — looking for suppliers of essential tools to an industry rather than competitors within it, evidence of market structure insight
  > "Usually, my favorite method for investing in high-growth areas where the competition is fierce is the pan-and-shovel technique." @line 185

- **title**: Patent protection as moat for biotech products — new biotech patents provide multi-year market exclusivity compared to generic competition in pharma
  > "Not only do these marvels of genetic engineering have the potential to cure many diseases that were incurable in the past, they also have the protection of new patents that won't expire for many years" @line 37

- **title**: Biotech market maturation via consolidation — cash-constrained biotech forced into mergers and partnerships to survive, reducing independent player pool
  > "biotech companies that are low on cash are being forced to enter into mergers, joint ventures, or licensing agreements with richer partners, mostly the pharmaceutical giants" @line 21

- **title**: Majority-stake acquisition as a valuation floor — when an acquirer pays a large premium for a controlling (but not full) stake, the remaining minority shares often carry built-in price support from the acquirer's standing option to buy the rest at a rising price
  > "Roche still has the option to acquire all of Genentech's remaining shares, at a prearranged price well above the market, that increases each quarter. This gives the stock some built-in support." @line 88-91

- **title**: Sympathy selloff risk even for established biotech leaders — even the most prosperous, earnings-generating biotech firm can see its market value halved overnight when an unrelated competitor fails a clinical trial, because the whole sector re-prices together
  > "the entire biotech sector takes a fall because one other company fails an important clinical test, and Amgen's stock price drops with the rest, in sympathy. What had been a $70 stock is promptly marked down to $35" @line 127-129

- **title**: Sector-level exception to the investor's edge doctrine — Lynch explicitly carves biotech out from his usual "anyone can be a successful stockpicker" thesis, conceding that its scientific/regulatory complexity favors professional fund management over amateur edge-based picking
  > "Although I spend much of my time telling people they can be successful stockpickers in almost any industry they follow, biotech is the exception." @line 146-148

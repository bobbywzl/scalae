## Concepts

- **title**: Business Model Survival Principle — Large companies with diversified assets, stable credit access, and recurring revenue sources can survive extended distress periods; small companies with asset-light models and limited cash face rapid extinction. Evidence: contrasts large companies that survived 1980s-1990s distress (Chrysler, GM, Sears) with hundreds of small companies that declared bankruptcy.
  > "Big companies got that way by being successful, and most of them own valuable assets: real estate, subsidiaries, cash in the till." @line 23

- **title**: False Recovery Illusion — Cost-cutting measures (budget reductions, store closures) can create short-term earnings gains that mask deeper, unresolved fundamental problems in customer retention and market positioning. Recovery requires sales growth, not just cost discipline.
  > "Once a troubled company faces up to its predicament (this, too, can take time), it will announce a plan for recovery, which usually involves taking an ax to the budget." @line 76

- **title**: Patience and Timing as Core Disciplines — Successful turnaround investing requires willingness to wait for concrete operational proof; premature entry before recovery signals materialize often results in permanent capital loss despite fundamental recovery later.
  > "You can afford to wait. In some cases, you can't afford not to wait." @line 73

- **title**: Asymmetric cost of missing bottoms vs. missing tops — the opportunity cost of buying a turnaround too late (after confirmation) is small compared to the capital-loss risk of buying too early on an unconfirmed recovery — test: Would waiting for the "show me" evidence only cost a few points of the eventual gain, versus how much a false start could cost if bought early?
  > "Missing the bottom on the way up won't cost you anything. It's missing the top on the way down that's always expensive." @line 180-182

- **title**: Recurring/serial turnarounds are normal, not disqualifying — a company can go through multiple genuine turnaround cycles across decades (Chrysler twice, Kmart at least twice, Pier 1 on its third), so a prior "failed" turnaround doesn't preclude a later real one — test: What concrete, current-cycle evidence (closed stores, revamped merchandise, rising sales) supports this specific turnaround attempt, independent of past cycles?
  > "Chrysler, for instance, has had two turnarounds since the early 1980s. Kmart has had at least two since its heyday in the 1960s. Pier 1, a perennial favorite of mine, is going through its third." @line 114-117

## Metrics

- **name**: Turnaround Return Multiples — realistic expectations for mature company recoveries range from 2x-3x return (double or triple); 10-20x returns are exceptional and typically only occur with young growth companies, not mature turnarounds
  > "But comebacks are rarely that lucrative. A double or a triple is normally a great result." @line 108

- **name**: Price Discount to Historical Peak — Sears traded at $45-46 in 1974-1995 despite peak of $61 in 1973; 16 years of zero appreciation during bull market signals failed recovery; use multi-decade windows for validation
  > "In 1990, Sears was selling for the same price it fetched 16 years earlier." @line 64

- **name**: Distressed-entry vs. confirmed-entry return multiple — formula: return multiple bought at point of maximum uncertainty (deathbed) vs. return multiple bought after confirming cash infusion, reading: 20x for the deathbed buyers vs. 10x for Lynch's own later, lower-risk entry — an explicit risk/reward tradeoff Lynch quantifies on himself

- **name**: Buyback size as a percent of shares outstanding — formula: announced repurchase / shares outstanding, reading: a 10% buyback is treated as meaningful confirmation of management's own confidence in the turnaround, not a token gesture

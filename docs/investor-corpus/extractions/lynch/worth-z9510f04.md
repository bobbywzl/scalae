## Concepts
- **title**: Structural arbitrage in mutual-to-public conversions: buyer acquires pre-existing equity at nominal cost — question: how does the absence of prior owners alter the mathematics of public offerings?
  > "That $20 million invested by the shareholders became their thrift-warming present to themselves; in effect, they were buying the business for nothing. And because their $20 million was injected into the S&L;, the book value doubled overnight, from $20 million to $40 million." @line 37
- **title**: Three-phase appreciation trajectory in thrift stocks: IPO pop, book value convergence, and acquisition premium — question: what sequential mechanisms sustain multi-year returns above market?
  > "Before the moratorium, the typical thrift stock would advance in three phases: the original pop, the gradual rise to book value, and a secondary pop when the thrift was acquired by a larger institution at a premium to its book value." @line 187
- **title**: Regulatory Cycle Reshaping a Structural Edge — a lucrative structural arbitrage draws regulatory scrutiny (insider self-dealing in free shares), triggers a moratorium, and re-emerges in a modified, less lucrative but still attractive form once new investor-protective rules are imposed.
  > "To stop this profiteering, the Office of Thrift Supervision slapped a moratorium on conversions in early 1994." @line 73-74
- **title**: Mutual Holding Company as a Weakened Version of the Same Trade — a hybrid conversion structure that preserves insider control and understates true earnings power and book value, producing a materially inferior version of the standard thrift-conversion opportunity.
  > "These deals are less attractive for investors. The real earnings power and real book value of the banks are understated, and the stocks don't have much lift." @line 166-168
- **title**: Industry Overcapacity as a Long-Run Consolidation Driver — a simple international comparison (branch/bank density in the U.S. vs. Great Britain) is used to argue the banking and thrift industry is structurally overbuilt, implying years of further merger-driven cost-cutting ahead.
  > "Great Britain has a population of 58 million and only seven different banks, while we've got almost five times the crowd and more than 20,000 banks__if you count the credit unions, thrifts, national banks, regional banks, and so on." @line 233-236
